\noindent This note sets out how deaths are to be added to the model of
cases, $R_t$ and reporting described in the companion note. It is a
specification, written before the model is built, so that the assumptions can
be checked. The model is to be tested on simulated data before it is fitted
to the outbreak (\S\ref{sec:tests}). \S\ref{sec:status} says what has been
built so far.

\section{What the reports show}

Each situation report gives an epidemic curve, described as being by date
of symptom onset, in which the confirmed cases of each day are split into
those alive and those who have died, as known at the date of the report. For
day $t$ of the curve and a report made on day $t_j$ we therefore have two
counts:
\begin{align*}
C(t\given t_j) &: \text{confirmed cases counted for day $t$, as now};\\
D(t\given t_j) &: \text{the number of those cases shown as dead in that report}.
\end{align*}
Both are read directly from the report: $D(t\given t_j)$ is the height of
the ``dead'' part of the bar for day $t$ and $C(t\given t_j)$ the
height of the whole bar, so $D(t\given t_j)\le C(t\given t_j)$ and
$C(t\given t_j)-D(t\given t_j)$ are shown as alive. 

Notation for what the reports show follows three rules.
\begin{itemize}
\item Everything about cases is a $C$ and everything about deaths a $D$.
\item $C(t\given t_j)$ and $D(t\given t_j)$ with no superscript are the
counts in the report, which are observed. A superscript marks a part of one
of them which is not observed: for example $C^{F}(t\given t_j)$ below is
the number of the $C(t\given t_j)$ cases which are fatal.
\item These are all running totals: what the report of $t_j$ shows. The
change in a total from the report before is written with a $\Delta$, for
example $\Delta C(t\given t_j)=C(t\given t_j)-C(t\given t_{j-1})$.
\end{itemize}
Three features of these data shape the model.
\begin{itemize}
\item A death is counted on the day its case is counted for, and the reports
do not say when the person died: we see only that the count of dead for a
day rose between two reports. For most cases that day is the day of onset.
For some of those who died it may instead be the day of death, where the
onset date was not known (\S\ref{sec:dated}); the reports do not say which
cases these are. So the day a death is counted for is neither reliably the
day of onset nor the day of death.
\item The dead are among the cases already counted. A death cannot be shown
before the case is itself in the report.
\item Deaths lag cases. Among onset days more than five weeks old, the case
count is about 79\% of its latest value 7 to 14 days after onset, and the
death count about 60\% of its latest value; at 21 to 28 days these are 97\%
and 87\%. The crude ratio $D/C$ in the latest report is 0.45 to 0.49 for
onset fortnights up to early September and 0.28 for the latest fortnight,
which is almost entirely this lag.
\end{itemize}
(These figures are from all 49 reports, for onset days from 12 July. They are
descriptive only.)

Counts are occasionally revised downwards. The case count for an onset day
falls between consecutive reports 41 times out of 2{,}129, and the death
count 18 times, never by more than 2. On 11 of the 18 occasions the case
count fell as well: a record which was removed had been shown as dead.

\paragraph{Onset days before the first report.} The first report is that of
12 July, and it already shows cases with onset from 20 April. For onset days
from 11 May to 11 July it shows 1{,}778 cases, 465 of them dead; the latest
report shows 2{,}220 cases and 961 dead for the same days. So more than half
of the deaths among these cases were recorded while the reports were being
made, and for the oldest of them this was two to four months after onset.
(Before 11 May the latest report shows 40 cases in all.)

\paragraph{The report of 6 September.} This report corrected the records of
onset days before 28 June, most of them more than ten weeks old. Compared
with the report of 5 September, which showed 1{,}613 cases and 639 dead for
those 69 onset days:
\begin{itemize}
\item the case count fell for 33 onset days, by 137 in all and by up to 13
for one day, and rose for 18 onset days, by 48 in all;
\item the death count fell for 27 onset days, by 58 in all and by up to 7
for one day, and rose for 18, by 31 in all;
\item for 6 onset days the death count fell by more than the case count did.
\end{itemize}
So the correction did more than remove records. Records were also added,
long after any case of those days would ordinarily be reported, and some
records shown as dead either became alive or were replaced by others. The
reports do not say what was done; moving cases from one onset day to
another would produce all three. In every other report the case count of
one of these onset days fell by at most 3 (once; otherwise at most 2) and
the death count by at most 1. No onset day from 25 June onwards was
affected.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

Throughout, $t$ is a day of onset and $t_1<t_2<\dots$ are the days of the
reports which include it. (Strictly, in everything to do with reports $t$ is
the day a case is \emph{recorded for}: a case is recorded for day $t$ if it
is one of the cases the reports count for that day, in $C(t\given t_j)$.
This is its day of onset except for the cases of \S\ref{sec:dated}; until
then the two can be read as the same.) The transmission model, the change points in $R_t$
and the reporting delay of cases are as in the companion note; they are
restated only where deaths alter them.

\subsection{Cases which will die}

Let $I(t)$ be the true number of cases with onset on day $t$. Of these,
$F(t)$ will eventually die:
\begin{equation}
F(t)\sim\bin\bigl(I(t),\ c_t\bigr),
\label{eq:cfr}
\end{equation}
where $c_t$ is the case fatality ratio of cases with onset on day $t$. The
remaining $N(t)=I(t)-F(t)$ survive. We call the two groups \emph{fatal} and
\emph{non-fatal} cases. Neither $F(t)$ nor $N(t)$ is observed.

Two points about what $c_t$ means. It is indexed by onset day, so a change in
$c_t$ is a change between cohorts of cases by when they fell ill. And it is a
ratio among confirmed cases with a known onset date, which are the only cases
in these data.

\subsection{Two delays for each case}

Each case, fatal or not, comes to be reported after a delay $X$ from onset.
For a non-fatal case this is the reporting delay of the existing model: a
gamma distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$,
$\theta=(\mu,\sigma)$, piecewise constant in the onset day.

Fatal cases may be found sooner or later than others. Their reporting delay
is that of non-fatal cases with time rescaled: a gamma distribution with
mean $\mu e^{\nu}$ and standard deviation $\sigma e^{\nu}$. The prior on the single parameter $\nu$ says that the two
delays are the same unless the data show otherwise:
\begin{equation}
\nu=0\ \text{with probability } \tfrac12,\qquad
\nu\sim N(0,\,0.5^2)\ \text{otherwise}.
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
The posterior probability that $\nu\ne 0$ is then a direct statement of the
evidence that fatal cases are reported differently, and when there is none
the model reduces to a common delay. (A normal prior with a small standard
deviation would also shrink $\nu$ towards zero, but would never return
exactly the common delay and gives no such probability.)

For a fatal case, the death comes to be recorded after a delay $Y$ from
onset, which has a gamma distribution with mean $\mu^{d}$ and standard
deviation $\sigma^{d}$. The delay $Y$ is the time from onset until the death is entered in the records from which the reports are made. It is therefore the time from onset to death plus however long the death then takes to be entered; the two parts are not separated. Its parameters
$\phi=(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ are also piecewise constant in the onset day,
with changes at unknown times (\S\ref{sec:changes}).

A fatal case is \emph{shown as dead} in the report of day $t'$ once it has
been reported \emph{and} its death recorded, that is, once
$\max(X,Y)\le t'-t$. If it has been reported but its death has not been
recorded ($X\le t'-t<Y$), it is shown as alive.

Days of the week are handled as for cases, but separately for the two
delays. The hazard of the reporting delay on day $k$ is multiplied by
$\psi_w$ and that of the delay to a death being recorded by $\psi^{d}_w$,
where $w$ is the day of the week of the next report. The two sets of
multipliers have the same prior but are estimated
separately: cases and deaths need not reach those who compile the reports by
the same route or on the same days.

\paragraph{Notation for the delays.} What the model needs from each delay
is the probability that the event has \emph{not} happened by the day of a
report, with the day-of-week multipliers allowed for. These follow the rule
used for the counts: $S^{C}$ is about cases being reported and $S^{D}$ about
deaths being recorded, and a second letter says which cases.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Symbol & Probability that, by day $t'$, \ldots \\
\midrule
$S^{C,N}(t\given t')$ & a non-fatal case with onset on day $t$ has not been reported \\
$S^{C,F}(t\given t')$ & a fatal case with onset on day $t$ has not been reported \\
$S^{D}(t\given t')$ & the death of a fatal case with onset on day $t$ has not been recorded \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
$S^{C,N}$ depends on $\theta$ and $\psi_w$, $S^{C,F}$ on $\theta$, $\nu$
and $\psi_w$, and $S^{D}$ on $\phi$ and $\psi^{d}_w$. When $\nu=0$,
$S^{C,F}=S^{C,N}$.

\subsection{What a report shows}

Leaving wrong records aside until \S\ref{sec:wrong}, the
$C(t\given t_j)$ cases of onset day $t$ in the report of day $t_j$ are some
of them fatal and the rest not:
\begin{equation}
C(t\given t_j)=C^{F}(t\given t_j)+C^{N}(t\given t_j),
\label{eq:observed}
\end{equation}
where $C^{F}(t\given t_j)$ is the number of fatal cases reported by day
$t_j$ and $C^{N}(t\given t_j)$ the number of non-fatal cases. The cases in
the report are then of three kinds:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & Number & Shown as \\
\midrule
Non-fatal cases reported so far & $C^{N}(t\given t_j)$ & alive \\
Fatal cases reported, death not yet recorded & $C^{F}(t\given t_j)-D(t\given t_j)$ & alive \\
Fatal cases reported, death recorded & $D(t\given t_j)$ & dead \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The counts $C$ and $D$ are observed. How the cases divide into $C^{F}$ and
$C^{N}$ is not: some of the cases shown as alive will die. One further thing
is not observed, which is described next: how the deaths newly shown in a
report divide between cases new to that report and cases already in the one
before.

\subsection{From one report to the next}

Write $p^{N}(t\given t_j)=1-S^{C,N}(t\given t_j)/S^{C,N}(t\given t_{j-1})$
for the probability that a non-fatal case not reported by $t_{j-1}$ is
reported by $t_j$, as in the existing model, and $p^{F}(t\given t_j)$ for
the same with $S^{C,F}$, for a fatal case.

The model says how each running total changes from the report of $t_{j-1}$
to the report of $t_j$. These changes are differences between what two
consecutive reports show. They are not events with their own dates: all
that is known is that something true of a case in the later report was not
true of it in the earlier one.

The cases newly in the report of $t_j$ are fatal or not:
\begin{align}
\underbrace{\Delta C^{F}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{fatal cases new}\\ \text{to the report}}}
 &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{F(t)-C^{F}(t\given t_{j-1})}_{\substack{\text{fatal cases not in}\\ \text{the report before}}},\
 \underbrace{p^{F}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{probability a fatal case}\\ \text{not reported by } t_{j-1}\\ \text{is reported by } t_j}}
 \Bigr),
 \label{eq:a}\\[1ex]
\underbrace{\Delta C^{N}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{non-fatal cases new}\\ \text{to the report}}}
 &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{N(t)-C^{N}(t\given t_{j-1})}_{\substack{\text{non-fatal cases not in}\\ \text{the report before}}},\
 \underbrace{p^{N}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{probability a non-fatal case}\\ \text{not reported by } t_{j-1}\\ \text{is reported by } t_j}}
 \Bigr),
 \label{eq:b}
\end{align}
and $\Delta C(t\given t_j)=\Delta C^{F}(t\given t_j)+\Delta C^{N}(t\given t_j)$ is
the observed rise in the case count.

The deaths newly shown in the report of $t_j$, $\Delta D(t\given t_j)$,
which are observed, belong to cases of two kinds, and the two are counted separately because
they have different probabilities:
\[
\Delta D(t\given t_j)=\dnew(t\given t_j)+\dold(t\given t_j).
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item $\dnew(t\given t_j)$: fatal cases which are in the report of $t_j$ for
the first time and are already shown as dead in it. Such a case was not in
the report of $t_{j-1}$ at all, so it adds one to the count of cases and one
to the count of deaths at once. This happens if its death had been recorded
by $t_j$, which has probability $1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)$: its being
unreported until now says nothing about whether the death has been recorded,
since the two delays are independent.
\item $\dold(t\given t_j)$: fatal cases which were in the report of
$t_{j-1}$, shown there as alive, and are shown as dead in the report of
$t_j$. There were $C^{F}(t\given t_{j-1})-D(t\given t_{j-1})$ such cases to
start with, and for each the death was not recorded by $t_{j-1}$ but is by
$t_j$.
\end{itemize}
So
\begin{align}
\dnew(t\given t_j) &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{\Delta C^{F}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{fatal cases new}\\ \text{to the report}}},\
 \underbrace{1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{probability the death}\\ \text{has been recorded by } t_j}}
 \Bigr),
 \label{eq:x}\\[1ex]
\dold(t\given t_j) &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{C^{F}(t\given t_{j-1})-D(t\given t_{j-1})}_{\substack{\text{fatal cases shown as alive}\\ \text{in the report before}}},\
 \underbrace{1-\frac{S^{D}(t\given t_j)}{S^{D}(t\given t_{j-1})}}_{\substack{\text{probability the death has been}\\ \text{recorded by } t_j \text{, given that it}\\ \text{had not been by } t_{j-1}}}
 \Bigr).
 \label{eq:y}
\end{align}
Only their sum $\Delta D(t\given t_j)$ is observed, not the two parts.

\paragraph{The first report.} For the first report which includes onset day
$t$, on day $t_1$, there is no report before. The equations then hold with
nothing yet reported and nothing yet recorded: the totals of the report
before are taken as zero,
$C^{F}(t\given t_0)=C^{N}(t\given t_0)=D(t\given t_0)=0$, and the
probabilities that nothing has yet happened as one,
$S^{C,N}(t\given t_0)=S^{C,F}(t\given t_0)=S^{D}(t\given t_0)=1$. So
\[
C^{F}(t\given t_1)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t),\ 1-S^{C,F}(t\given t_1)\bigr),\qquad
C^{N}(t\given t_1)\sim\bin\bigl(N(t),\ 1-S^{C,N}(t\given t_1)\bigr),
\]
and, since no case was shown as alive in a report before,
$\Delta D^{\mathrm{old}}(t\given t_1)=0$ and
\[
D(t\given t_1)\sim\bin\bigl(C^{F}(t\given t_1),\ 1-S^{D}(t\given t_1)\bigr).
\]

For example, suppose a report shows 10 cases for an onset day, 3 of them
dead, and the next report shows 12 cases, 5 of them dead. Then
$\Delta C=2$ and $\Delta D=2$. The two new deaths could both be among the 7
cases shown as alive before ($\dnew=0$, $\dold=2$); or both of the 2 new
cases could have arrived already dead ($\dnew=2$, $\dold=0$); or one of each.
The reports do not say which, and the model sums over the possibilities.

Two consequences follow, and both are checks on the construction.
\begin{itemize}
\item When $\nu=0$ the case counts alone behave exactly as in the existing
model: adding \eqref{eq:a} and \eqref{eq:b}, new cases are
$\bin(I(t)-C(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p^{N}(t\given t_j))$.
\item A fatal case is shown as dead by day $t'$ with probability
$[1-S^{C,F}(t\given t')]\,[1-S^{D}(t\given t')]$, the
probability that both delays have elapsed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deaths recorded for the day of death}\label{sec:dated}

Some of those who died may be recorded for their day of death and not their
day of onset (for example, where a person was identified only at or after
death and the onset date is not known). Such a case is recorded for the
wrong day: one later than its onset by the time from onset to death. The
model therefore keeps two sets of counts, one by true day of onset and one
by the day cases are recorded for, and moves these cases between them.
Below, a fatal case is \emph{dated by death} if it is recorded for its day
of death and \emph{dated by onset} if it is recorded for its day of onset.

\paragraph{Inputs.} The data cannot tell us which cases these are, so two
things are supplied and not estimated:
\begin{itemize}
\item $f_t$: the fraction of fatal cases with onset on day $t$ which are
dated by death (a single value, or one for
each day; $f_t=0$ gives the model above);
\item $h_z$, $z=1,2,\dots,z_{\max}$: the distribution of the time in days
from onset to death for these cases. It is a gamma distribution with
supplied mean and standard deviation, rounded up to a whole number of days,
so that $h_z$ is the probability the gamma distribution gives to times
between $z-1$ and $z$ days. The time is therefore at least one day: a
person who dies on their day of onset is recorded for the correct day
whichever date is used, and is not counted as dated by death.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{By day of onset.} $I(t)$, $F(t)$ and $c_t$ keep their meanings:
true cases, fatal cases and the case fatality ratio \emph{by true day of
onset}. The renewal model and \eqref{eq:cfr} are unchanged, so that
transmission and the case fatality ratio are both on the correct days. Of
the fatal cases of onset day $t$,
\begin{equation}
F^{d}(t)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t),\ f_t\bigr)
\label{eq:dated}
\end{equation}
are dated by their death, and these die after times from onset drawn
independently from $h$: writing $W(t,z)$ for the number of them who die $z$
days after onset,
\begin{equation}
\bigl(W(t,1),\dots,W(t,z_{\max})\bigr)\sim
\operatorname{multinomial}\bigl(F^{d}(t),\ h\bigr).
\label{eq:lags}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{By day recorded for.} The cases recorded for day $t$ are then
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & Number & \\
\midrule
Non-fatal cases with onset on day $t$ & $N(t)=I(t)-F(t)$ & \\
Fatal cases with onset on day $t$, dated by onset & $F(t)-F^{d}(t)$ & \\
Fatal cases who died on day $t$, dated by death & $G(t)=\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t-z,z)$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
so that $\tilde{F}(t)=F(t)-F^{d}(t)+G(t)$ fatal cases and
$\tilde{I}(t)=N(t)+\tilde{F}(t)$ cases in all are recorded for day $t$.

$F^{d}(t)$ and $G(t)$ both count fatal cases dated by death, but group them by
different days. $F^{d}(t)$ groups them by onset: it is the number with onset on
day $t$, and these are recorded for later days, not for day $t$. $G(t)$
groups them by death: it is the number who died on day $t$, whatever their
day of onset, and these are the ones recorded for day $t$. The two are linked
by $W$, since $F^{d}(t)=\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t,z)$.

A fatal case can be recorded for day $t$ in only two ways, which the
expression for $\tilde{F}(t)$ adds up. Either its onset was day $t$ and it
is dated by onset: of the $F(t)$ fatal cases with onset on day $t$, the
$F^{d}(t)$ dated by death are recorded for a later day, which leaves
$F(t)-F^{d}(t)$. Or it died on day $t$ and is dated by death, its onset being
earlier: there are $G(t)$ of these. No case is counted twice, because a
fatal case is dated either by onset or by death, and none of the $F^{d}(t)$
cases is among the $G(t)$, because the time from onset to death is at least
one day.

For example, suppose 10 fatal cases have onset on 9 August, of which 4 are
dated by death, and that 5 fatal cases with earlier onsets, dated by death,
die on 9 August. Then $F=10$, $F^{d}=4$ and $G=5$ for 9 August, and
$\tilde{F}=10-4+5=11$ fatal cases are recorded for that day, although only
10 fatal cases truly had onset on it. The 4 are recorded for whichever later
days they die on, and are counted in $G$ for those days.

It is
these, not $F(t)$ and $I(t)$, which the counts $C(t\given t_j)$ and
$D(t\given t_j)$ come to include. In the equations for reports, $t$ is therefore the day cases are recorded
for; how \eqref{eq:a}--\eqref{eq:y} are altered is set out below, first
exactly and then in the approximate form which is used. When $f_t=0$ the two
sets of counts are the same and those equations are unchanged.

\paragraph{How these cases are reported.} A case dated by its death is
assumed to be shown as dead from the first report it appears in. How long
it takes to first appear, counted from its date of death, is set by a
switch:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{supplied} (intended for use): the delay from the date of death
to first appearing in a report has its own gamma distribution, with supplied
mean and standard deviation. Write $S^{C,d}(t\given t')$ for the
probability that such a case, which died on day $t$, has not been reported
by day $t'$ ($d$ for dated by death). A person
identified at death is likely to enter the reports quickly, so this would
usually be shorter than the reporting delay of other cases. It is not
estimated and does not change over time. The day-of-week multipliers
$\psi_w$ of the reporting delay apply to it;
\item \emph{same as other fatal cases}: the delay from the date of death is
that of other fatal cases from their date of onset, $S^{C,d}=S^{C,F}$.
This needs no further input, but there is little reason to believe it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The exact version.} The two kinds of fatal case recorded for day
$t$ are reported at different speeds, so strictly each must be followed
separately. Write $C^{F,o}(t\given t_j)$ and $C^{F,d}(t\given t_j)$ for the
numbers reported by day $t_j$ of those dated by onset and those dated by
death, and $D^{o}(t\given t_j)$ for the number of the former shown as dead.
From the report of $t_{j-1}$ to the report of $t_j$,
\begin{align}
\text{dated by onset, new to the report}\quad
 & \Delta C^{F,o}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t)-F^{d}(t)-C^{F,o}(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p^{F}(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:ao}\\
\text{dated by death, new to the report}\quad
 & \Delta C^{F,d}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(G(t)-C^{F,d}(t\given t_{j-1}),\
 1-\frac{S^{C,d}(t\given t_j)}{S^{C,d}(t\given t_{j-1})}\Bigr),
 \label{eq:ad}\\
\text{of the $\Delta C^{F,o}(t\given t_j)$, shown as dead}\quad
 & \Delta D^{o,\mathrm{new}}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(\Delta C^{F,o}(t\given t_j),\ 1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:xo}
\end{align}
with $\dold(t\given t_j)$ as in \eqref{eq:y} for the cases dated by onset
which were in the report before, shown as alive. A case dated by death is shown as
dead in the first report it appears in; one dated by onset is shown as dead
in its first report only if its death has been recorded by then, and
otherwise is shown as alive for now and changes to dead in a later report.
The reports then show
\[
C(t\given t_j)=C^{N}(t\given t_j)+C^{F,o}(t\given t_j)+C^{F,d}(t\given t_j),\qquad
D(t\given t_j)=D^{o}(t\given t_j)+C^{F,d}(t\given t_j).
\]
This is exact, but it has two unobserved numbers for each day and report,
$C^{F,o}$ and $C^{F,d}$, where the model without these cases has one. The
sampling of these numbers (\S\ref{sec:fitting}) is the slowest part of the
fitting and would be several times slower, by a factor of roughly the
number of cases dated by death recorded for a day.

\paragraph{The approximation used.} The model as built follows only the
total, $C^{F}(t\given t_j)=C^{F,o}(t\given t_j)+C^{F,d}(t\given t_j)$, as before. To
do this it does not use the number $G(t)$ of cases dated by death as a fixed
number. It treats each of the $\tilde{F}(t)$ fatal cases recorded for day
$t$ as dated by death with probability
\[
\tilde{f}_t=\frac{G(t)}{\tilde{F}(t)},
\]
independently of the others. The probability that such a case has not been
reported by day $t_j$ is then a mixture over the two kinds,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{S}^{C,F}(t\given t_j)=(1-\tilde{f}_t)\,S^{C,F}(t\given t_j)
+\tilde{f}_t\,S^{C,d}(t\given t_j),
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:ao} and \eqref{eq:ad} are replaced by a single equation for
the fatal cases new to the report, of either kind,
\begin{equation}
\Delta C^{F}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(\tilde{F}(t)-C^{F}(t\given t_{j-1}),\
1-\frac{\tilde{S}^{C,F}(t\given t_j)}{\tilde{S}^{C,F}(t\given t_{j-1})}\Bigr).
\label{eq:amix}
\end{equation}
Each of these is one of those dated by death with probability
\begin{equation}
\pi(t\given t_j)=\frac{\tilde{f}_t\,\bigl[S^{C,d}(t\given t_{j-1})-S^{C,d}(t\given t_j)\bigr]}
{\tilde{S}^{C,F}(t\given t_{j-1})-\tilde{S}^{C,F}(t\given t_j)},
\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
which is large in the first reports after day $t$ if these cases are
reported quickly, and small later. It is then shown as dead in its first
report with certainty if it is dated by death, and with probability
$1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)$ if it is dated by onset, so that \eqref{eq:xo}
and the cases dated by death together give
\begin{equation}
\dnew(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(\Delta C^{F}(t\given t_j),\
\pi(t\given t_j)+\bigl(1-\pi(t\given t_j)\bigr)\bigl[1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)\bigr]\Bigr)
\label{eq:xdated}
\end{equation}
for the number new to the report and shown as dead. Equation \eqref{eq:y} is
unchanged, since a fatal case shown as alive cannot be dated by its death.
These are \eqref{eq:a}--\eqref{eq:y} with $\tilde{F}(t)$ for $F(t)$ and two
probabilities altered. The delay $\phi$ is that of the fatal cases dated by
onset only. With the switch at \emph{same as other fatal cases},
$\tilde{S}^{C,F}=S^{C,F}$ and $\pi(t\given t_j)=\tilde{f}_t$.

\paragraph{What the approximation changes.} For a single fatal case,
\eqref{eq:mixture}--\eqref{eq:xdated} are exact: a case taken at random from
those recorded for day $t$ is dated by death with probability
$\tilde{f}_t$. The approximation is in treating the cases as independent.
Suppose 10 fatal cases are recorded for a day, of which $G(t)=3$ are dated
by death. Given this, the exact version gives no probability to reports
which would need four or more cases dated by death. The approximation
treats each of the 10 as dated by death with probability $3/10$, so that
the number is 3 on average but could be 2 or 5, and such reports are only
less probable. The expected counts in each report are the same in the two
versions; the approximation allows somewhat more variation about them. It
matters most when counts are small and the two kinds are reported at very
different speeds.

$G(t)$ is itself unobserved and uncertain, and is informed by the reports
in either version, so the approximation does not change what the model can
represent: it weakens how strongly the reports bear on the number of cases
dated by death. Whether this biases any estimate is checked on simulated
data (\S\ref{sec:tests}), which are generated exactly, each case being of
one kind or the other. If it does, the exact version is the remedy.

\paragraph{The start of the series.} Some fatal cases had their onset
before the first day analysed but died after it. If such a case is dated by
death, it is recorded for its day of death, which is inside the period
analysed. For example, with a first day of 12 July, a person with onset on
8 July who died on 15 July and is dated by death is among the cases the
reports count for 15 July.

Such a case must not be taken as a case with onset on 15 July. For onsets
inside the period analysed the model moves a case dated by death back to its
day of onset, but here it cannot: the onset is before the first day, where
the case counts are taken as known and are not changed. So for each of the
first $z_{\max}$ days there is one further unobserved count, $G_0(t)$: the
number of fatal cases recorded for day $t$ whose onset was before the first
day. These are part of $G(t)$,
\[
G(t)=G_0(t)+\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t-z,z),
\]
where the sum is over onsets within the period, and they are left out of
the true cases with onset on day $t$.

The prior for $G_0(t)$ is Poisson, with mean equal to the number of such
cases expected: for each earlier day, the known cases, times the case
fatality ratio $c_1$, times the fraction dated by death, times the
probability under $h$ of dying on day $t$; summed over the earlier days.
After the first $z_{\max}$ days there are none. If no earlier cases are
supplied, there are taken to be none at all. (In updating $c_1$ the
dependence of this prior on it is ignored; it involves only the first few
weeks.)

\paragraph{What is not known.} A case dated by death which has not yet died
is in no report, and once it has died it is recorded for its day of death, not
its day of onset. Recent onset days are therefore missing cases for a reason
other than delay in reporting. The model allows for this: \eqref{eq:lags}
includes deaths after the last day for which there are reports, and the
number of such cases for each recent onset day is a further unobserved
count. But the data say nothing about it, so estimates for recent
days lean on the supplied $f_t$ and $h$. Fitting with a range of values for
both is therefore part of any analysis which uses them.

\subsection{Wrong records}\label{sec:wrong}

The existing model lets records be added to a count in error and later
removed: between two reports $n(t\given t_j)\sim\pois(\eta\,I(t)\,
[S^{C,N}(t\given t_{j-1})-S^{C,N}(t\given t_j)])$ wrong records are
added, and each wrong record present is removed with probability
$1-(1-\rho)^{t_j-t_{j-1}}$. Since removed records are sometimes dead, a wrong
record is now of one of two kinds: each new wrong record is shown as dead
with probability $\delta$ and as alive otherwise. With $E^{A}$ and $E^{D}$
the numbers of wrong records shown as alive and as dead, the counts in a
report include them:
\[
C(t\given t_j)=C^{N}(t\given t_j)+C^{F}(t\given t_j)+E^{A}(t\given t_j)+E^{D}(t\given t_j),
\qquad
D(t\given t_j)=D^{G}(t\given t_j)+E^{D}(t\given t_j),
\]
where $D^{G}(t\given t_j)$ is the number of genuine fatal cases shown as
dead. Without wrong records $D^{G}=D$, which is why the deaths of genuine
cases were written as $D$ above; with them, $D^{G}$ is not observed and
takes the place of $D$ in \eqref{eq:x} and \eqref{eq:y}.
As now, the total $E^{A}+E^{D}$ for an onset day is limited to a small
number (one more than the largest fall seen in a count of cases or of
deaths). Where some fatal cases are dated by death (\S\ref{sec:dated}), the
$I(t)$ in the rate at which wrong records are added is the number of cases
recorded for day $t$, $\tilde{I}(t)$. A fall in the death
count with no fall in the case count (7 of the 18 falls) needs no further
mechanism: it is a wrong record shown as dead being removed while a genuine
case is added.

\subsection{Onset days before the first report}\label{sec:early}

Nothing in the model requires the first report which includes an onset day
to be made on that day. For an onset day $t$ before the first report, $t_1$
is the day of the first report and the equations of ``The first report''
above apply as they stand: the cases and deaths it shows are those whose
delays had elapsed by $t_1$. Such a day says little about the reporting
delay of cases, since nearly all its cases are in the first report it
appears in. It says more about deaths. The deaths recorded from one report
to the next, through \eqref{eq:y}, show how long after onset deaths go on
being recorded, and once that has stopped the share of cases shown as dead
is the case fatality ratio for that day.

The analysis therefore starts at the onset day of 11 May, not at the day of
the first report as in the companion note. Cases with earlier onset (40 in
the latest report) are supplied as known to the renewal equation, as there.

\paragraph{The reporting delay of onset days first seen late.} An onset
day first seen weeks after onset says almost nothing about the reporting
delay: nearly all its cases are already in the first report which includes
it. The model nonetheless gives every onset day a reporting delay, and if a
change (\S\ref{sec:changes}) is placed among such days the delay of the
segment before it is drawn almost from its prior. In a first fit to the
outbreak one chain did this, giving the onset days before 28 June a mean
reporting delay of 50 to 60 days where the others had 8.

So no change in the reporting delay is allowed among these days. Take the
onset days within 7 days of the first report as the first which inform the
delay (about half of a day's cases are reported within a week). The first
segment of the reporting delay must then run from the first onset day
analysed to at least 14 days (the minimum length of any segment) after the
first of those: here, to 18 July or later, the first report being that of
12 July. The onset days of May and June then have the reporting delay of
early July. This is an assumption, not an estimate: if cases with onset in
May were reported more slowly than those of July, the model cannot see it.
It applies to the delay from onset to a case being reported only. Changes
in the case fatality ratio and in the delay to a death being recorded are
allowed on any day, since deaths among the cases of an onset day go on
being recorded for weeks after it is first seen.

\paragraph{Cases added long after onset.} Following an onset day for months
shows something a shorter series does not: now and then a case is added to
an onset day long after every other case of that day was reported. For
example the count for onset day 14 May stood at 7 from the report of 6
September and rose to 8 on 13 September, four months after onset. Under a
gamma reporting delay with a mean of about a week this has a probability
too small to compute, and with starting values of that kind the fit cannot
begin. The existing model has an option for this, which is now allowed for
alongside deaths: a share $\omega$ of cases, fatal or not, have a reporting
delay which is exponential with mean $m$ in place of the gamma, so that
\[
S^{C,N}(t\given t')=(1-\omega)\,S_{\mathrm{gamma}}(t'-t)+\omega\,e^{-(t'-t)/m},
\]
with $\omega\sim\betad(1,19)$ and $m\sim$ gamma with mean 40 and sd 30 days
for this outbreak. $\omega$ and $m$ are the same for all onset days and are
updated by a random walk given the paths, like the day-of-week multipliers.
The delay to a death being recorded has no such tail: where deaths are
recorded slowly the model has to say so through $(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ of
that segment.

\subsection{A report which corrects many records}\label{sec:correction}

The wrong records of \S\ref{sec:wrong} are few and are removed one at a
time. The report of 6 September is a different thing: for onset days before
28 June it removed 137 records, added 48 and changed which records were
shown as dead. The model of the sections above cannot follow counts across
such a report. Two inputs describe it: the day it was made, $t_c$, and the
set $\mathcal{T}_c$ of onset days it corrected (here 6 September, and the
onset days from 11 May to 27 June). Both are supplied, having been read
from the data. They are not estimated.

\paragraph{What is done.} For an onset day $t\in\mathcal{T}_c$ the reports
made before $t_c$ are left out, and the report of $t_c$ is treated as the
first report of that day. Nothing else changes: the equations of ``The
first report'' apply with $t_1=t_c$, as for any onset day first seen some
time after onset (\S\ref{sec:early}), and the later reports follow from it
in the usual way. For onset days not in $\mathcal{T}_c$ all reports are
used.

In other words, the corrected onset days are taken as the records show them
from the correction on. Here those onset days are 71 to 118 days old at
$t_c$ and there are 20 reports from then to 1 October. Nearly all their
cases have been reported and nearly all their deaths recorded by $t_c$, so
$I(t)$ and $F(t)$ are close to the counts of the latest report, and the
case fatality ratio of these days is close to the share shown as dead. The
model still allows for the few cases and deaths added after $t_c$, and for
wrong records among them.

\paragraph{What this gives up.} The reports of the corrected onset days
made before $t_c$ (here from 12 July to 5 September) showed their deaths
being recorded over many weeks. Leaving them out means the corrected onset
days say almost nothing about the delays, least of all the delay to a death
being recorded. For this outbreak the delays in May and June are then
learned only through the changes of \S\ref{sec:changes}: from the onset
days from 28 June on, and from the little that is still added to the
corrected days after 6 September. If deaths were recorded more slowly for
onsets in May and June than later, the model has little with which to see
it. This matters less for the case fatality ratio of those days than it
might, since their deaths are almost all recorded by the latest report
whatever the delay was.

\paragraph{A check which uses the earlier reports.} To see what is given
up, one further fit keeps the reports made before $t_c$, as a second and
separate series of reports for each corrected onset day. This
\emph{uncorrected series} is modelled by the same equations and the same
delays as the reports from $t_c$ on, but with its own totals $I^{u}(t)$ and
$F^{u}(t)$ in place of $I(t)$ and $F(t)$: the numbers of cases and of fatal
cases which the uncorrected records would have come to. These have flat
priors,
\[
I^{u}(t)\sim\operatorname{uniform}\{0,\dots,I_{\max}\},\qquad
F^{u}(t)\given I^{u}(t)\sim\operatorname{uniform}\{0,\dots,I^{u}(t)\},
\]
and enter neither the renewal equation nor \eqref{eq:cfr}. So the
uncorrected series informs only the delays and the parameters of the wrong
records ($\eta$, $\rho$ and $\delta$, which it shares), and says nothing
about $c_t$ or the number of cases.

This is a check and not the main analysis for two reasons. It treats the
two series as independent given the delays, although they are mostly the
same cases, so some of the timing of their deaths is used twice and the
delay to a death being recorded is made to look better known than it is.
And it assumes the uncorrected records were reported, and their deaths
recorded, with the delays of the onset day they were then shown for, which
is not so for a record that was moved or should not have been there. If the
two fits give much the same delays, the simpler one loses little. If they
differ, the difference shows how much the conclusions about May and June
rest on reports which were later corrected.

\paragraph{Not done.} The correction itself is not modelled (so many wrong
records removed, so many cases added, so many records changed from dead to
alive, for each onset day). That needs five unobserved counts for each
onset day which the data can barely tell apart: one more record removed and
one more added leave the counts the same.

\subsection{Changes at unknown times}\label{sec:changes}

Three quantities are piecewise constant in the onset day, each with its own
change points and the same prior on them as in the companion note (each day
starts a new segment with probability $p$, $p\sim\betad(1,T)$, and segments
have a minimum length).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Quantity & Prior within a segment & Minimum segment & Status \\
\midrule
$R_t$ & gamma & 7 days & existing \\
Reporting delay $(\mu,\sigma)$ & hierarchical log-normal & 14 days & existing \\
Case fatality ratio $c_t$ & $\betad(1,1)$ & 14 days & new \\
Delay to death recorded $(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ & hierarchical log-normal & 14 days & new \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The remaining new parameters are $\delta\sim\betad(1,1)$, the day-of-week
multipliers $\psi^{d}_w$ of the delay to a death being recorded (same prior
as $\psi_w$), and $\nu$ with prior \eqref{eq:nu}. The fractions $f_t$ and
the distribution $h$ are inputs.

\section{A backlog of deaths cleared in calendar time (proposed)}\label{sec:backlog}

This section sketches a model which is not yet built. It is prompted by how
the fitted model, set out briefly below, fails to predict the deaths later
reports add to onset days which are weeks or months old.

\subsection{What the reports show}

Each report adds deaths to onset days of every age. Taking the delay of a
death to be the time from its onset to the first report which shows it as
dead, three things stand out (Figure~\ref{fig:backlogwave}).
\begin{itemize}
\item The deaths of early onset days go on being added for months, at a
roughly steady rate. Deaths with onset in June were added at 5.8, 3.1 and
2.9 a day by the reports of July (from 12 July), August and September, and
then almost stopped (0.4 a day in those of 1 to 7 October). Deaths with
onset in May were added at about one a day throughout, and still are.
\item The share of a cohort's deaths that is slow falls with onset date.
Of the deaths shown within 35 days of onset, those first shown more than 21
days after onset were 18 to 20\% for onsets from 13 July to 9 August and
9\% for onsets from 24 August to 6 September.
\item By month of report, the delays of the deaths added to old onset days
move to the right one day for every day of calendar time, with the
earliest onset days at the front. A hump at 60 to 90 days in the reports of
August is at about 90 days in those of September: largely the same onset
days of June, a month older.
\end{itemize}
The last point is what a backlog cleared in calendar time would produce. If
each report clears some of the deaths still waiting, whatever their age,
the delays of the deaths it adds are the report date less their onset
dates. The distribution of delays in a report is then the profile of the
remaining backlog over onset days, shifted by the date of the report.

\subsection{The model fitted so far, and why it fails}

The fitted model (option (f) of the fits to the outbreak) differs from the
model of \S\ref{sec:model} in its delay to a death being recorded. That
delay is the time from onset to death, $T$, with the supplied distribution
$h$ of \S\ref{sec:dated}, followed by a delay $U$ from death to the death
being recorded. With $S^{U}(d\given t')$ the probability that a death on day
$d$ is not yet recorded by day $t'$,
\begin{equation}
S^{D}(t\given t')=\sum_{z>t'-t}h_z+\sum_{z\le t'-t}h_z\,S^{U}(t+z\given t'),
\label{eq:sdbacklog}
\end{equation}
and in option (f)
\[
S^{U}(d\given t')=(1-\omega^{d})\,S_{\mathrm{gamma}}(t'-d\given\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})
+\omega^{d}\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{s=d+1}^{t'}\frac{\psi^{d}_{w(j(s))}\,e^{\varepsilon_{j(s)}}}{m^{d}}\Bigr),
\qquad \varepsilon_j\sim N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^{2}),
\]
where $j(s)$ is the report whose interval contains day $s$ and $w(j)$ the
day of the week of report $t_j$. A share
$\omega^{d}$ of deaths are thus recorded at a rate which is constant, $1/m^{d}$,
apart from a multiplier for each report. The gamma part and $\omega^{d}$,
$m^{d}$ are the same for all onset days to 31 July; $(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ may
change after it.

This is already a rate set by calendar time, but with two restrictions which
the reports contradict.
\begin{enumerate}
\item One share $\omega^{d}$ for all onset days. Early onset days have been
exposed to the clearing rate for longest, so in the model they have the
least left. In the reports they had the most.
\item Report effects that are independent around a constant rate. Nothing
links the rate of one report to that of the next, so the model explains
deaths added to old onset days by a few large effects (multipliers of 25
to 60 in the fits of August and early September) which empty the backlog.
\end{enumerate}
The consequence is in the fits to earlier reports. Each fit from 1 August
to 2 September puts the deaths still to be shown for onset days more than
35 days old at about zero; the reports later added about 140 to 210. Over the
week after each fit, the fits of 9 August to 9 September expected 28 to
50\% of the deaths that were added, and almost none of those added more
than 35 days after onset.

\subsection{The proposed model}

Each fatal case dies on day $t+T$, $T\sim h$, as now. Its death then goes
one of two ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item With probability $1-b_t$ it is recorded promptly, after a gamma delay
from death with parameters $(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ and the day-of-week
multipliers $\psi^{d}_w$, as now.
\item With probability $b_t$ it joins a \emph{backlog}. A death in the
backlog is recorded on day $s$ with hazard $\lambda_s$, the same for all
onset days.
\end{itemize}
So, in \eqref{eq:sdbacklog},
\begin{equation}
S^{U}(d\given t')=(1-b_t)\,S_{\mathrm{gamma}}(t'-d\given\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})
+b_t\,e^{-\Lambda(d,t')},\qquad
\Lambda(d,t')=\sum_{s=d+1}^{t'}\lambda_s,
\label{eq:backlog}
\end{equation}
for a death on day $d$ of a case with onset on day $t$. The rest of the
model is unchanged: \eqref{eq:sdbacklog} enters the triggered and misdated
parts as $S^{D}$ does now, and for a case dated by death the backlog share is
that of its day of onset.

\paragraph{The backlog share.} $b_t$ varies with onset day, as a random walk
on the logit scale over onset weeks $k$:
\[
\operatorname{logit} b_{(k+1)}=\operatorname{logit} b_{(k)}+\xi_k,\qquad
\xi_k\sim N(0,\tau_b^{2}),
\]
with, for example, $\operatorname{logit}b_{(1)}\sim N(\operatorname{logit}0.2,\,1)$
and $\tau_b\sim$ half-normal$(0,0.5)$. Change points in $b_t$, as for the
other quantities of \S\ref{sec:changes}, are the alternative; the random
walk is preferred because the reports suggest a steady fall rather than a
step.

\paragraph{The clearing rate.} $\lambda_s$ is constant between consecutive
reports, $\lambda_s=\lambda_j$ for $t_{j-1}<s\le t_j$, and $\log\lambda_j$ is a
random walk over reports with variance proportional to the time between
them:
\[
\log\lambda_j=\log\lambda_{j-1}+\zeta_j,\qquad
\zeta_j\sim N\bigl(0,\ \tau_\lambda^{2}\,(t_j-t_{j-1})/7\bigr).
\]
Before the first report a single rate $\lambda_0$ applies, with, for example,
$\log\lambda_0\sim N(\log(1/60),\,1)$; only the first report informs it, and
only through the share of each onset day's deaths shown by then. The rate
can therefore rise, stay high through a period of reconciliation and fall,
which independent effects around a constant cannot do. It replaces $m^{d}$
and the effects $\varepsilon_j$.

\paragraph{Why the two can be told apart.} Once the prompt deaths of an onset
day are recorded, the deaths it gains between reports $t_{j-1}$ and $t_j$
have expectation close to
\[
B(t\given t_{j-1})\Bigl(1-e^{-\lambda_j\,(t_j-t_{j-1})}\Bigr),\qquad
B(t\given t')=b_t\,F(t)\sum_{z}h_z\,e^{-\Lambda(t+z,\,t')},
\]
where $B(t\given t')$ is the backlog of onset day $t$ left at day $t'$. This
is a factor for the onset day times a factor for the report. Every report
informs $\lambda_j$ through all the old onset days at once, and every onset
day informs $b_t$ through all the reports which add to it. The travelling
wave of Figure~\ref{fig:backlogwave} is this product: the profile of $B$ over
onset days, read off at the delays $t_j-t$.

\paragraph{Option (f) as a special case.} With $b_t=\omega^{d}$ for all $t$ and
$\lambda_s=\psi^{d}_{w(j(s))}e^{\varepsilon_{j(s)}}/m^{d}$, \eqref{eq:backlog} is
option (f). The proposal changes two things: the share varies with onset
day, and the rate is persistent rather than independent from report to
report.

\paragraph{Projections.} Beyond the last report $\lambda$ follows its random
walk from its last value. The backlog of recent onset days is the part the
data cannot yet see: their deaths join it but are not cleared until later,
so $b_t$ for the last few weeks rests on the random walk from earlier weeks.
This is the main source of uncertainty in projected deaths, and the model
states it, where option (f) states with confidence that the backlog is
empty.

\paragraph{Fitting.} Given $b$ and $\lambda$, onset days are independent, as
now, so the sampler for the counts is unchanged. $b$ and $\lambda$ are
updated by Metropolis given the paths, in blocks of consecutive weeks or
reports, in place of the updates of $\omega^{d}$, $m^{d}$ and $\varepsilon_j$.
$\Lambda$ is a cumulative sum over calendar days, computed once per update.

\paragraph{An alternative not proposed first.} The deaths of June onsets
were added at an almost constant rate and then stopped, which suggests a
clearing capacity rather than a hazard: some number $K_j$ of backlog deaths
cleared by each report, shared among onset days in proportion to what each
has waiting. In the form above that links all onset days in each report;
it is easiest to write with the backlog made explicit
(\S\ref{sec:backlogexplicit}). A hazard which rises as the backlog shrinks
can imitate it, so the hazard model is to be tried first.

\paragraph{Checks.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Before building it: a Poisson log-linear model for the deaths added to
onset days at least 35 days old, with a factor for onset week and a factor
for report interval and an offset for the interval's length. If this
describes the table of additions by onset week and report, the product
structure above is adequate. If the residuals show the backlog of one cohort
cleared before the next, the capacity model is the better description.
\item After: one-step-ahead predictions from fits to earlier reports, of the
deaths each later report adds and their delays from onset, and the backlog
each fit believes remains against what was later added. These are the
checks which option (f) fails.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The backlog made explicit}\label{sec:backlogexplicit}

The model above sums over which fatal cases join the backlog: every fatal
case has the mixture \eqref{eq:backlog} as its delay. An equivalent version
keeps that split as unobserved counts, as the exact form of
\S\ref{sec:dated} keeps the cases dated by death. It has the same posterior
for $b$, $\lambda$ and the counts, since given the parameters the cases are
independent and summing over the split gives back the mixture. What changes
is how the sampler moves, and which extensions are easy to write.

\paragraph{The partition.} Of the fatal cases with onset on day $t$ which are
dated by onset,
\[
F^{B}(t)\given F(t),F^{d}(t)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t)-F^{d}(t),\ b_t\bigr)
\]
join the backlog and the other $F^{P}(t)=F(t)-F^{d}(t)-F^{B}(t)$ are
recorded promptly. The cases dated by death are split in the same way, with
the share of their day of onset: each $W(t,z)$ of \eqref{eq:lags} becomes
$W^{P}(t,z)+W^{B}(t,z)$, and the cases recorded for day $t$ dated by death are
$G^{P}(t)+G^{B}(t)$, with $G^{B}(t)=\sum_{z\ge1}W^{B}(t-z,z)$.

\paragraph{Two simpler delays.} Each group has a delay without a mixture:
\[
S^{D,P}(t\given t')=\sum_{z>t'-t}h_z+\sum_{z\le t'-t}h_z\,S_{\mathrm{gamma}}(t'-t-z\given\mu^{d},\sigma^{d}),
\qquad
S^{D,B}(t\given t')=\sum_{z>t'-t}h_z+\sum_{z\le t'-t}h_z\,e^{-\Lambda(t+z,\,t')},
\]
so that $S^{D}=(1-b_t)\,S^{D,P}+b_t\,S^{D,B}$. The chain of binomials of
\S\ref{sec:model} is applied to each group with its own delay. Every hidden
count of deaths newly shown in a report (such as $\dnew$ and $\dold$) is
split into a prompt and a backlog part, and only their sum is constrained by
the reports. The cases dated by death are treated the same way, with the
delay from death of their group.

\paragraph{Updating the clearing rate.} Given the split, $\lambda_j$ enters
only the binomials of backlog deaths in the interval from $t_{j-1}$ to
$t_j$. For an onset day more than $z_{\max}$ days old at $t_{j-1}$ every
backlog death has already happened, and each still waiting is recorded in
the interval with the same probability $1-e^{-\lambda_j(t_j-t_{j-1})}$. These
days, which are most of those the backlog shows up in, enter the conditional
of $\lambda_j$ through two totals only: the backlog deaths waiting at
$t_{j-1}$ and those recorded by $t_j$. Younger onset days add a few terms of
their own. An update of $\lambda_j$ then costs little, where in the summed
form it means recomputing the delays of every onset day after $t_{j-1}$.

\paragraph{Updating the backlog share.} Given $F^{B}$, $b_t$ is the
probability of a binomial: conjugate within a segment if $b_t$ has change
points (a beta prior gives a beta conditional), and a cheap Metropolis step
for each week if it is a random walk on the logit scale.

\paragraph{Updating the split.} The new unobserved counts are $F^{B}(t)$ (and
$W^{B}$) and the split of each count of deaths newly shown. A proposal moves
one fatal case of an onset day from one group to the other, together with
the report in which its death was first shown, keeping the totals the
reports constrain. As with the cases dated by death, these moves are where
mixing may be slow: for an early onset day nearly all deaths are already
shown, so which of them were in the backlog is weakly informed, and is held
together only through $b_t$ and $\lambda$.

\paragraph{The backlog as an output.} The backlog of onset day $t$ still
waiting at day $t'$, $B(t\given t')$, is a sampled count rather than an
expectation. It can be reported, projected and compared with the deaths
later reports add, which is the check option (f) fails.

\paragraph{A clearing capacity.} The explicit form also makes the capacity
model of \S\ref{sec:backlog} easy to write. Take the onset days more than
$z_{\max}$ days old at $t_{j-1}$, as above. Let $N_j$ be their backlog deaths
waiting at $t_{j-1}$, and $K_j$ the number recorded by
$t_j$. Under the hazard model each waiting death is recorded independently,
so $K_j\sim\bin\bigl(N_j,\,1-e^{-\lambda_j(t_j-t_{j-1})}\bigr)$, and given
$K_j$ the deaths recorded are spread over onset days as a draw without
replacement from those waiting (a multivariate hypergeometric distribution,
in proportion to what each onset day has waiting). A capacity model keeps
that allocation and changes only the distribution of $K_j$: for example
$K_j\sim\min\bigl\{N_j,\ \pois(\kappa_j\,(t_j-t_{j-1}))\bigr\}$ with
$\log\kappa_j$ a random walk over reports, so that the number cleared does
not grow with the size of the backlog. The two models then differ in a
single term for each report, and can be compared directly.

\section{Assumptions to check}\label{sec:assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The two delays of a fatal case are independent.} In truth a
case found at or near death has both delays short. Independence is what
makes \eqref{eq:x} simple, and is assumed to begin with. The posterior
predictive check for it is the share of reported cases shown as dead by time
since onset, which in the data is 0.41 within 3 days of onset, 0.33 at 3 to
7 days and 0.46 after six weeks. If the fitted model cannot reproduce this,
a dependent mechanism will be explored; the cases of \S\ref{sec:dated} are
one such mechanism, since for them the two delays coincide.
\item \emph{The reporting delays of fatal and non-fatal cases differ, if at
all, by a rescaling of time} (the parameter $\nu$), the same throughout the
outbreak.
\item \emph{A wrong record is as likely to be shown as dead whenever it is
added.}
\item \emph{Cases dated by death are shown as dead from the first report
they appear in} (\S\ref{sec:dated}). The fraction of fatal cases dated in
this way, the distribution of their time from onset to death and the
distribution of their delay from death to first appearing in a report are
supplied, not estimated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What can and cannot be learned}\label{sec:identifiability}

A recent fall in the case fatality ratio and deaths not yet recorded look
the same in the latest reports. For onset days in the last three to four
weeks the death count is far from complete, so $c_t$ there is informed
mostly by the delay $\phi$ estimated from earlier onset days. We therefore
expect that a change in $c_t$ in the last three to four weeks cannot be
detected, and that a change in $\phi$ and a change in $c_t$ at about the same
time will be hard to tell apart. The simulations in \S\ref{sec:tests} are
designed to measure both.

Deaths also inform recent case numbers a little, through \eqref{eq:cfr}:
many early deaths among few reported cases make a small $I(t)$ unlikely.

\section{Fitting}\label{sec:fitting}

The sampler extends the existing one (Metropolis within Gibbs). The new
unobserved counts are the fatal cases $F(t)$ and, for each onset day, the
path over reports of the fatal cases reported $C^{F}(t\given t_j)$ and of the
wrong records $E^{A},E^{D}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{$F(t)$, with the path summed out.} Once every case of an onset
day has been reported, the fatal cases among those reported are all the
fatal cases, $C^{F}=F(t)$, so neither can move while the other is held fixed.
$F(t)$ is therefore updated first with the path summed out: a change of up
to three is proposed and accepted with the Metropolis probability, using the
probability of the reports of that onset day given $I(t)$ and $F(t)$. This
probability is the result of filtering forwards over the reports.
\item \emph{Paths.} For each onset day the path of
$(C^{F},E^{A},E^{D})$ over reports is then drawn exactly given everything else,
by sampling backwards. The state space is small because $C^{F}$ can rise between
two reports by no more than the case count does. Steps 1 and 2 are the
slowest part of the fitting and are done in compiled code; states whose
probability given the reports so far is below $10^{-12}$ of the largest are
dropped.
\item \emph{$I(t)$ and $F(t)$ given the paths.} Given the paths, the
likelihood separates into a term in $F(t)$ from \eqref{eq:a}, a term in
$N(t)$ from \eqref{eq:b} and the renewal terms in $I(t)$. $I(t)$ is drawn
from its discrete conditional with $F(t)$ summed out, and $F(t)$ is then
drawn given $I(t)$, so the pair is drawn jointly. This is what moves the
recent onset days, for which many cases are still to be reported.
\item \emph{Case fatality ratio.} Given $F$ and $I$, the segments of $c_t$
are drawn exactly, with the value in each segment integrated out
(beta-binomial), as for $R_t$ under the Poisson model. The values are then
drawn from their beta conditionals.
\item \emph{Delay to death recorded.} Given the paths, $\phi$ enters only
through \eqref{eq:x} and \eqref{eq:y}. Its segments are updated by the
split, merge and shift moves already used for the reporting delay.
\item \emph{Reporting delay.} As now, with the likelihood from \eqref{eq:a}
and \eqref{eq:b}.
\item \emph{$R_t$ and $\kappa$:} as now.
\item \emph{Wrong records.} Given the paths, the wrong records added and
removed between reports are known. $\delta$ is drawn from its beta
conditional and $\eta$ from its gamma conditional; $\rho$ is updated by a
random walk on the logit scale.
\item \emph{Day-of-week multipliers.} $\psi_w$ and $\psi^{d}_w$ are each
updated by a random walk, given the paths: $\psi_w$ through \eqref{eq:a},
\eqref{eq:b} and the wrong records added, $\psi^{d}_w$ through \eqref{eq:x}
and \eqref{eq:y}.
\item \emph{Cases dated by death} (\S\ref{sec:dated}). Given the paths,
four kinds of change of a single case are proposed, many times in each
iteration, and each is accepted with the Metropolis probability:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{relabel}: one fatal case recorded for day $t$ changes from a
case with onset on day $t$, dated by onset, to a case with onset on day
$t-z$ which died on day $t$, dated by death, or the reverse. This moves one
true case between onset days $t$ and $t-z$;
\item \emph{change of onset}: one case dated by death and recorded for day
$t$ changes its time from onset to death, which moves one true case between
two onset days;
\item \emph{death after the last day}: one fatal case dated by death which
has not yet died is added to, or removed from, a recent onset day;
\item \emph{onset before the first day}: one fatal case recorded for one of
the first days changes from a case with onset on that day to one with
earlier onset, or the reverse.
\end{itemize}
None of these alters the number of cases, or of fatal cases, recorded for
any day. The Metropolis probability involves the renewal model,
\eqref{eq:cfr}, \eqref{eq:dated}, \eqref{eq:lags} and, for the first and
last kinds, the reports of day $t$ through $\tilde{f}_t$. The other updates
are as above with $\tilde{F}(t)$ and the altered probabilities; for days
long enough ago to be taken as fully reported, the true cases with onset on
day $t$ are the latest reported count less $G(t)-F^{d}(t)$.
\item \emph{Uncorrected series} (\S\ref{sec:correction}; only in the fit
which uses the reports made before the correction). $I^{u}(t)$,
$F^{u}(t)$ and the paths of the uncorrected series are updated by steps 1
to 3, with the flat priors in place of the renewal terms and
\eqref{eq:cfr}. In steps 5, 6, 8, 9 and 11 the uncorrected series of a
corrected onset day is one more series of reports with the delays of that
day.
\item \emph{$\nu$.} Given the paths, $\nu$ enters only through
\eqref{eq:a} (and \eqref{eq:pi}, where cases dated by death have their own
delay). From $\nu=0$, a value drawn from the normal part of the prior is
proposed; from $\nu\ne0$, either $\nu=0$ or a small change is proposed,
with equal probability. Each is accepted with the Metropolis--Hastings
probability. The share of draws with $\nu=0$ estimates the posterior
probability that the two delays are the same.
\end{enumerate}

\section{A relaxed version}\label{sec:relaxed}

The existing model has a relaxed version, written in Stan, in which true
cases are continuous and nothing is sampled as a whole number, so that it
can be fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo or by faster approximate methods.
Deaths have been added to it. It is an independent check on the sampler of
\S\ref{sec:fitting} and a much faster way to fit.

\paragraph{The unobserved counts can be removed.} The obstacle is the
unobserved counts: the fatal cases $F(t)$ and, for each report, the fatal
cases $C^{F}(t\given t_j)$ among those reported. There are thousands of
them. But when fatal cases are reported as others are ($\nu=0$) and none
are dated by death, they can be summed out exactly. Each case is in one of
three states which the reports show: not yet reported, shown as alive, or
shown as dead. Because the two delays are independent, the chance that a
case moves from one state to another between two reports does not depend on
how long it has been where it is. The counts in a report then depend only on
those in the report before:
\begin{align}
\Delta C(t\given t_j) &\sim\bin\bigl(I(t)-C(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p^{N}(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \nonumber\\
\dnew(t\given t_j) &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{\Delta C(t\given t_j)}_{\substack{\text{cases new}\\ \text{to the report}}},\
 \underbrace{c_t\,\bigl[1-S^{D}(t\given t_j)\bigr]}_{\substack{\text{fatal, and the death}\\ \text{recorded by } t_j}}
 \Bigr),\label{eq:lumpnew}\\[1ex]
\dold(t\given t_j) &\sim\bin\Bigl(
 \underbrace{C(t\given t_{j-1})-D(t\given t_{j-1})}_{\substack{\text{cases shown as alive}\\ \text{in the report before}}},\
 \underbrace{\frac{c_t\,\bigl[S^{D}(t\given t_{j-1})-S^{D}(t\given t_j)\bigr]}
 {1-c_t+c_t\,S^{D}(t\given t_{j-1})}}_{\substack{\text{fatal, and the death recorded by } t_j\text{,}\\ \text{given shown as alive at } t_{j-1}}}
 \Bigr).\label{eq:lumpold}
\end{align}
Everything on the right is observed except $I(t)$, $c_t$ and the delays.
These equations give the same probability of the reports as
\eqref{eq:cfr} and \eqref{eq:a}--\eqref{eq:y} with $F(t)$ and
$C^{F}(t\given t_j)$ summed over; this has been checked numerically
(agreement to seven significant figures).

\paragraph{What the relaxed version includes.} The case fatality ratio and
the delay to a death being recorded each change at unknown onset days, up
to a maximum number of changes which is set in advance, as $R_t$ and the
reporting delay already do in the relaxed version. The days of the changes
are summed over exactly, those of the two quantities together. Wrong
records shown as dead are summed over by a small recursion, for onset days
on which a count of deaths falls. The delay to a death being recorded has
its own day-of-week multipliers.

\paragraph{What it leaves out, and why.} It has no minimum length of
segment. It does not yet have a different delay for fatal cases ($\nu$) or
cases dated by death. With either, the probability of one onset day's
reports depends on the segments of several quantities at once (for example
on the case fatality ratio \emph{and} the reporting delay), and with cases
dated by death the true cases of one day depend on the case fatality ratio
of earlier days. The exact summing over the days of changes then no longer
separates into small pieces. Both can be added with further approximations,
which have not been made.

\paragraph{A first fit.} On the simulated outbreak in which the case
fatality ratio halves (\S\ref{sec:status}), the relaxed version fitted by
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (two chains of 300 iterations, 7.5 minutes) gave a
ratio of 0.52 (0.49--0.54) falling to 0.28 (0.25--0.30), against 0.52 and
0.27 from the sampler and 0.54 and 0.25 realised; a delay to a death being
recorded of mean 12.5 (12.0--13.0) and sd 7.2 (6.8--7.7) days, against a
truth of 12 and 7; and true cases inside the 90\% interval on all of the
last 14 onset days. The fastest approximate method (a normal approximation
at the mode) took 30 seconds and gave similar estimates of these, but put
the probability of a change in the delay to a death being recorded at 0.38
where there is none (0.04 by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo).

\section{Tests on simulated data}\label{sec:tests}

Data are simulated case by case: each case is given a reporting delay,
whether it dies and, if so, a delay to the death being recorded, and the
reports are counted from these. The tests, each over several simulated
outbreaks and with at least three chains checked by split $\hat{R}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item constant $c_t$ and $\phi$: are $c_t$, $\phi$ and recent $F(t)$
recovered, with no changes inferred?
\item a change in $c_t$ at 6, 4, 3 and 2 weeks before the end of the series:
how old must a change be to be found?
\item a change in $\phi$ with $c_t$ constant: is it attributed to $\phi$ and
not to $c_t$?
\item changes in both;
\item data simulated with the two delays of a fatal case \emph{dependent}:
how far wrong is the model when assumption 1 fails?
\item data simulated with fatal cases reported sooner, and with the same
delay: is $\nu$ recovered in the first case and is $\nu=0$ preferred in the
second?
\item data simulated with a fraction of fatal cases dated by death, fitted with the right inputs, with $f_t=0$, and with a wrong
fraction, a wrong distribution of the time to death or a wrong delay from
death to first appearing in a report: are $R_t$, $c_t$ and
$\phi$ recovered with the right inputs, and how sensitive are they to wrong
ones?
\item a first report made some weeks after the first onset day analysed
(\S\ref{sec:early}), with the case fatality ratio and the delay to a death
being recorded constant, and with each changing before the first report:
are the values for the early onset days recovered?
\item a report which corrects the records of early onset days by removing
some, adding some and moving some between onset days
(\S\ref{sec:correction}), with and without a change in $c_t$ among the
corrected onset days, fitted with the reports before the correction left
out for those onset days, and fitted with them as an uncorrected series:
are the cases, $c_t$ and $\phi$ of the corrected onset days recovered, and
what does the uncorrected series add? (Fitting as if there had been no correction is not
an option: the falls are too large for the wrong records of
\S\ref{sec:wrong}.)
\item cases, $R_t$ and the reporting delay: are these estimated at least as
well as by the model without deaths?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Status}\label{sec:status}

\begin{itemize}
\item Built and unit-tested (package branch \texttt{deaths}): the whole
model of \S\ref{sec:model} and the sampler of \S\ref{sec:fitting}, with the
two sets of counts of \S\ref{sec:dated} in the approximate form and, as a
switch, in the exact form; the relaxed version of \S\ref{sec:relaxed}; and a
simulator with a change in any of the delays or the case fatality ratio,
fatal cases reported at a different speed, cases dated by death and wrong
records (but not day-of-week effects). The sampling of the paths has been
checked against an independent calculation over every possible path for
small examples, with and without wrong records, and the changes to the
cases dated by death against the full probability of the model. These are
checks that the code does what the equations say, not that the estimates
are right.
\item The exact form for cases dated by death is slower than the
approximate form by about a factor of two (3.0 against 1.5 seconds an
iteration on the simulated outbreaks, with wrong records allowed for), not
the five to ten first guessed. The changes which move a case between the
two kinds must sum over the split of the reports, since that split fixes
how many of each kind there are.
\item The tests on simulated data are run by the pipeline of the paper
repository (targets named \texttt{deaths\_sim}).
\item The tests on simulated data have been run: \S\ref{sec:results}. They
replace an earlier quick check of seven variants on one outbreak.
\item A first fit to the outbreak has been run and has not converged:
\S\ref{sec:fit}.
\item Onset days before the first report (\S\ref{sec:early}) need no change
to the model: a fit starting 14 days before the first report runs. Leaving
out the reports made before a correction (\S\ref{sec:correction}) needs no
change either. The uncorrected series used as a check there and the heavier
tail of the reporting delay of \S\ref{sec:early} are built and unit-tested,
and the simulator can
make a report which removes records, adds missing cases and moves cases
between onset days.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results on simulated data}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{What was run.} 32 variants of how an outbreak is reported, each
on the same three simulated outbreaks of 60 onset days (about 40 cases a
day; reporting delay of mean 8 and sd 6 days; delay to a death being
recorded of mean 12 and sd 7 days; case fatality ratio 0.4 unless stated).
Each was fitted by two chains of 600 iterations, the first 300 discarded.
Twenty variants are ones the model should handle and twelve are ones where
it should struggle, either because the data say little or because the
model is told something wrong. Figure \ref{fig:validation} shows every
variant and outbreak; Figures \ref{fig:pages} onwards show the fits to the
first outbreak for every variant (Figure \ref{fig:validationnew} has those
with a correcting report).

\paragraph{Cautions.} Three outbreaks is few. The same three are used for
every variant, so a miss which belongs to one outbreak appears in many rows
of Figure \ref{fig:validation}. Coverage of a quantity which is constant
over the outbreak is then 0, 1/3, 2/3 or 1 and cannot be compared with
90\%. The chains are short and there are two, not the three or more of
\S\ref{sec:tests}. The heavier tail of the reporting delay
(\S\ref{sec:early}) is not in any variant. Of 192 chains one failed (one
chain of the approximate form for cases dated by death, which stopped with
true cases fewer than the cases reported); its variant has one chain for
that outbreak.

\paragraph{Where the model should do well.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Case fatality ratio.} The posterior median is within 0.02 of
the truth in all twenty variants (mean absolute error 0.001 to 0.019). A
change is found in every draw wherever there is one, and in 1\% to 12\% of
draws where there is none.
\item \emph{Delay to a death being recorded.} The mean is within about half
a day of the truth where it is constant (0.15 to 0.6 days; 0.9 when the
case fatality ratio is 0.1 and deaths are few). A change in it is found in
every draw in both variants which have one, with an error of 0.6 to 1.0
days in the mean. Its 90\% intervals miss more often than they should in
several variants, so they are probably too narrow.
\item \emph{Fatal cases reported at a different speed.} The ratio is
estimated as 0.51 (0.44 to 0.59) against 0.5, and 1.41 (1.31 to 1.53)
against 1.5. Where the truth is 1, a ratio of exactly 1 has 55\% to 88\% of
draws.
\item \emph{Wrong records.} $\eta$ 0.027 against 0.03, $\rho$ 0.33 against
0.3, $\delta$ 0.47 against 0.4.
\item \emph{True cases and fatal cases.} For the latest 14 onset days the
90\% intervals contain the truth on 86\% to 95\% of days for cases and 83\%
to 98\% for fatal cases. For earlier days, 87\% to 99\%, and 82\% to 89\%
where some fatal cases are dated by death.
\item \emph{Cases dated by death.} With the right inputs the share dated
by death is recovered (0.27 and 0.28 against 0.27) by both the exact and
the approximate form.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Onset days first seen late, and a correcting report.} These are
the variants added for the outbreak (\S\ref{sec:early},
\S\ref{sec:correction}).
\begin{itemize}
\item With the first report on day 25, the case fatality ratio is as well
estimated as with a report every day (error 0.007 to 0.011). A halving of
it on day 15, among onset days first seen ten or more days after onset, is
found in every draw. A fall in the delay to a death being recorded on day
15 is also found in every draw, with an error of 0.6 days in the mean, but
the intervals for that delay contained the truth on under half the days.
\item With a report on day 45 which corrects onset days 1 to 20, leaving
out the earlier reports of those days gives a case fatality ratio with
error 0.008 and a mean delay to a death being recorded with error 0.52
days. Using the earlier reports as a separate series gives 0.007 and 0.48:
no better. Its intervals for the delay contained the truth less often (47\%
of days against 76\%), which is what using the same deaths twice would do.
This supports leaving the earlier reports out. A halving of the case
fatality ratio among the corrected onset days was found in every draw.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where the model should struggle.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A change ten days before the end.} A halving of the case
fatality ratio is found in 3\% of draws, and the fatal cases of the latest
14 days are then overestimated (intervals contain the truth on 43\% of
days). A rise in $R_t$ ten days before the end is also missed: the error in
recent $R_t$ is 0.11 against 0.02 elsewhere.
\item \emph{The case fatality ratio and the delay to a death being recorded
changing together.} The two chains disagree (split $\hat R$ 2.8) and the
error in the mean delay is 2.5 days. This is the confounding of
\S\ref{sec:identifiability}.
\item \emph{A gradual fall} in the case fatality ratio is fitted as one
step, with error 0.04.
\item \emph{A death recorded only after the case is reported} (assumption 1
false). The case fatality ratio is unaffected (error 0.01), but the fatal
delay ratio is estimated as 0.59 where it is 1 and the reporting delay is
wrong by 1.8 days in the mean, outside its interval in all three outbreaks.
\item \emph{Cases dated by death, told wrongly.} Fitting as if there were
none leaves the case fatality ratio nearly right (error 0.02) but the
intervals for true cases on earlier days contain the truth on 29\% of
days. Being told 10\% or 50\% where it is 30\%, or too long a time to
death, costs little (coverage of earlier cases 79\% to 83\%). Being told
that these cases are reported like other fatal cases gives an error of 2.9
days in the delay to a death being recorded and a fatal delay ratio of
0.63.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Things to look at.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $R_t$ is constant at 1 in most variants. Its posterior median
differs from 1 by 0.02 in one outbreak and 0.04 in another, and in the
second the interval excludes 1 in nearly every variant. The earlier quick
check gave 0.97 to 0.98. This may be the outbreaks or a small bias; it has
not been traced.
\item The intervals for the case fatality ratio missed the truth for one
outbreak in about a third of the variants, by about 0.02. Three outbreaks
cannot say whether those intervals are too narrow.
\item Agreement between chains for the reporting delay was poor in a few
variants (split $\hat R$ up to 1.28), and for the delay to a death being
recorded where it changes (1.24).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Time.} A chain of 600 iterations took 12 to 15 minutes in most
variants with eight running at once (about 1.3 seconds an iteration), 28
minutes with the exact form for cases dated by death, 34 to 98 minutes
with all features together, and 47 to 109 minutes where cases reach several
hundred a day.

\section{Fit to the outbreak: first run, not converged}\label{sec:fit}

\paragraph{What was run.} The model was fitted to all reports up to 1
October, for onset days from 11 May (144 onset days; 6{,}428 cases and
2{,}821 deaths in the latest report). For the onset days from 11 May to 27
June only the reports from 6 September on are used (\S\ref{sec:correction}).
No fatal cases are taken to be dated by death. The heavier tail of the
reporting delay is included (\S\ref{sec:early}), with wrong records,
day-of-week effects and a negative binomial renewal model. Three chains of
1{,}000 iterations were run from different starting values, the first 500
discarded; each took about three hours.

\paragraph{The chains do not agree, so these are not results.} Table
\ref{tab:fit} gives each monitored quantity with its split $\hat R$ and the
median of each chain, and Figure \ref{fig:fitchains} shows the chains
separately. Split $\hat R$ is above 1.1 for 15 of the 18 quantities and
above 2 for 7. The pooled estimates of Figure \ref{fig:fit} mix chains
which are in different places, and its intervals should not be read as
posterior intervals.

\paragraph{Where the chains agree.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The case fatality ratio rises at the end of May, from about 0.37
(0.33--0.43) to 0.52 or more. A change is found in every draw of every
chain.
\item Fatal cases are reported no faster or slower than other cases.
\item The reporting delay of recent onset days has a mean of 7.6 days.
\item $\eta$ and $\delta$.
\item The reproduction number falls from about 1.2 at the end of June to
about 0.9 by 1 October. (Its values before June rest on very few earlier
cases.)
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where they do not.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The delay to a death being recorded, and with it the case
fatality ratio.} Chains 1 and 2 have a mean delay of about 40 days for
onset days up to mid or late July and about 19 days after, with a case
fatality ratio of 0.52. Chain 3 has no change in the delay, a mean of 68
days throughout and a case fatality ratio of 0.57 rising to 0.63. Slower
recording with more deaths still to come, and faster recording with fewer,
fit the reports about equally well as far as these chains show: this is the
confounding of \S\ref{sec:identifiability}, which the tests also showed
(\S\ref{sec:results}). The total of fatal cases follows: 3{,}530, 3{,}750
and 4{,}390 in the three chains, against 2{,}821 deaths in the latest
report.
\item \emph{The reporting delay before 28 June.} Chain 1 has a mean of 50
to 60 days for these onset days and the others about 8. These are the
corrected onset days, first used 71 or more days after onset, so the
reports say almost nothing about their reporting delay
(\S\ref{sec:correction}).
\item \emph{The tail.} The mean of the long delay, $m$, is 23, 76 and 97
days in the three chains.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Two things which look wrong.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The share of cases with a long reporting delay, $\omega$, is 0.18
(0.15--0.23), against a prior with mean 0.05. The tail was meant for the
occasional case added months late; a share of this size means it is
standing in for something else, and it will affect the estimates of recent
true cases.
\item In every chain the fatal cases exceed the deaths in the latest report
by a quarter or more, including for onset days in June and July. That may
be so, but it rests on a delay to a death being recorded of 40 days or more
which the chains do not agree on.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Next.} Longer chains, to see whether the two explanations are
two modes or one slowly explored posterior; a look at what the tail is
absorbing; and the two checks which are still running (a fifth of fatal
cases dated by death; the reports made before 6 September as a separate
series).

\section{Fits to earlier reports: second run, not converged}\label{sec:nowcast}

\paragraph{What was run.} The model of \S\ref{sec:fit} was changed in one
way: no change in the reporting delay is allowed before 19 July
(\S\ref{sec:early}). It was fitted five times, each time to the reports up
to a given date only: 1 August, 20 August, 5 September (the day before the
correcting report), 15 September and 1 October. The estimates made at each
earlier date can then be set against what the report of 1 October shows.
Three chains of 1{,}000 iterations were run for each date, the first 500
discarded. All three chains were started from the same plausible values,
scaled by 1, 0.9 and 1.1: a case fatality ratio of 0.5, a mean delay to a
death being recorded of 22 days and a mean reporting delay of 7.6 days.
Chains started together cannot show a second mode which none of them
finds, so agreement here is weaker evidence than agreement from spread
starts.

\paragraph{Convergence.} No date converged (Table~\ref{tab:nowcastrhat}).
The reporting delay is now steady, at 7.6 to 8.4 days for every date, and
its $\hat{R}$ is below 1.1 for three of the five dates. What does not
converge is the pair which \S\ref{sec:identifiability} warns of: the case
fatality ratio and the delay to a death being recorded. On the reports to 1
October one chain has a mean delay of 117 days and a case fatality ratio of
0.54 for the first onset days, the other two 36 to 39 days and 0.36 to
0.37, although all three started together. All five dates are being rerun
with 3{,}000 iterations. Table~\ref{tab:nowcastestimates} gives the estimates of
every monitored quantity at each date and Table~\ref{tab:nowcastallrhat}
their $\hat{R}$.

\paragraph{Estimates against the report of 1 October.}
Figure~\ref{fig:nowcasts} and Table~\ref{tab:nowcast} compare, for onset
days from 28 June, the estimate made at each date with the count in the
report of 1 October. That report is not the truth: its counts for recent
onset days, deaths above all, are still rising. Since the chains have not
converged, the intervals are not posterior intervals.

From the reports to 1 August the estimates for the latest week are good:
458 cases (386 to 545) against 424 in the report of 1 October, where 87 had
been reported, and 192 fatal cases (155 to 237) against 191, where 33 had
been reported. For onset days one to four weeks back the cases are 10 to
15\% too high. At the later dates the estimates are too high throughout.
Cases are 10 to 40\% above the report of 1 October. The model puts 17 to
26\% of cases in the long tail of the reporting delay, with a mean of 57 to
95 days, and so expects many more late reports than arrived. Fatal cases
estimated in September are 30 to 50\% above the report of 1 October even
for onset days more than four weeks old. Those fits have a case fatality
ratio near 0.6 and a mean delay to a death being recorded of 50 to 90 days
(Figure~\ref{fig:nowcastparameters}): the reported deaths are read as a
small part of a large total still to come.
