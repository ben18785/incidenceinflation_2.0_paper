\noindent This note sets out how deaths are to be added to the model of
cases, $R_t$ and reporting described in the companion note. It is a
specification, written before the model is built, so that the assumptions can
be checked. The model is to be tested on simulated data before it is fitted
to the outbreak (\S\ref{sec:tests}). \S\ref{sec:status} says what has been
built so far.

\section{What the reports show}

Each situation report gives an epidemic curve, described as being by date
of symptom onset, in which the confirmed cases of each day are split into
those alive and those who have died, as known at the date of the report. For
day $t$ of the curve and a report made on day $t_j$ we therefore have two
counts:
\begin{align*}
C(t\given t_j) &: \text{confirmed cases counted for day $t$, as now};\\
M(t\given t_j) &: \text{the number of those cases shown as dead in that report}
\ (M \text{ for mortality}).
\end{align*}
Both are read directly from the report: $M(t\given t_j)$ is the height of
the ``dead'' part of the bar for onset day $t$ and $C(t\given t_j)$ the
height of the whole bar, so $M(t\given t_j)\le C(t\given t_j)$ and
$C(t\given t_j)-M(t\given t_j)$ are shown as alive. Capital letters with a
report day, $C(t\given t_j)$ and $M(t\given t_j)$, are always observed
counts. Lower-case letters introduced below ($r$, $s$, $m$, $a$, $b$, $x$,
$y$) are unobserved.
Three features of these data shape the model.
\begin{itemize}
\item A death is counted on the day its case is counted for, and the reports
do not say when the person died: we see only that the count of dead for a
day rose between two reports. For most cases that day is the day of onset.
For some of those who died it may instead be the day of death, where the
onset date was not known (\S\ref{sec:dated}); the reports do not say which
cases these are. So the day a death is counted for is neither reliably the
day of onset nor the day of death.
\item The dead are among the cases already counted. A death cannot be shown
before the case is itself in the report.
\item Deaths lag cases. Among onset days more than five weeks old, the case
count is about 79\% of its latest value 7 to 14 days after onset, and the
death count about 60\% of its latest value; at 21 to 28 days these are 97\%
and 87\%. The crude ratio $M/C$ in the latest report is 0.45 to 0.49 for
onset fortnights up to early September and 0.28 for the latest fortnight,
which is almost entirely this lag.
\end{itemize}
(These figures are from all 49 reports, for onset days from 12 July. They are
descriptive only.)

Counts are occasionally revised downwards. The case count for an onset day
falls between consecutive reports 41 times out of 2{,}129, and the death
count 18 times, never by more than 2. On 11 of the 18 occasions the case
count fell as well: a record which was removed had been shown as dead.

\section{Model}\label{sec:model}

Throughout, $t$ is a day of onset and $t_1<t_2<\dots$ are the days of the
reports which include it. (Strictly, in everything to do with reports $t$ is
the day a case is \emph{recorded for}: a case is recorded for day $t$ if it
is one of the cases the reports count for that day, in $C(t\given t_j)$.
This is its day of onset except for the cases of \S\ref{sec:dated}; until
then the two can be read as the same.) The transmission model, the change points in $R_t$
and the reporting delay of cases are as in the companion note; they are
restated only where deaths alter them.

\subsection{Cases which will die}

Let $I(t)$ be the true number of cases with onset on day $t$. Of these,
$F(t)$ will eventually die:
\begin{equation}
F(t)\sim\bin\bigl(I(t),\ c_t\bigr),
\label{eq:cfr}
\end{equation}
where $c_t$ is the case fatality ratio of cases with onset on day $t$. The
remaining $N(t)=I(t)-F(t)$ survive. We call the two groups \emph{fatal} and
\emph{non-fatal} cases. Neither $F(t)$ nor $N(t)$ is observed.

Two points about what $c_t$ means. It is indexed by onset day, so a change in
$c_t$ is a change between cohorts of cases by when they fell ill. And it is a
ratio among confirmed cases with a known onset date, which are the only cases
in these data.

\subsection{Two delays for each case}

Each case, fatal or not, comes to be reported after a delay $X$ from onset.
For a non-fatal case this has survival function $S_\theta$ (a gamma
distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, piecewise
constant in the onset day), the reporting delay of the existing model.

Fatal cases may be found sooner or later than others. Their reporting delay
is that of non-fatal cases with time rescaled: a gamma distribution with
mean $\mu e^{\nu}$ and standard deviation $\sigma e^{\nu}$, written
$S_{\theta,\nu}$. The prior on the single parameter $\nu$ says that the two
delays are the same unless the data show otherwise:
\begin{equation}
\nu=0\ \text{with probability } \tfrac12,\qquad
\nu\sim N(0,\,0.5^2)\ \text{otherwise}.
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
The posterior probability that $\nu\ne 0$ is then a direct statement of the
evidence that fatal cases are reported differently, and when there is none
the model reduces to a common delay. (A normal prior with a small standard
deviation would also shrink $\nu$ towards zero, but would never return
exactly the common delay and gives no such probability.)

For a fatal case, the death comes to be recorded after a delay $Y$ from
onset, with survival function $S_\phi$ (a gamma distribution with mean
$\mu^{d}$ and standard deviation $\sigma^{d}$). The delay $Y$ runs from onset
to the death being known to those who compile the reports: it includes both
the time from onset to death and any delay in recording it. Its parameters
$\phi=(\mu^{d},\sigma^{d})$ are also piecewise constant in the onset day,
with changes at unknown times (\S\ref{sec:changes}).

A fatal case is \emph{shown as dead} in the report of day $t'$ once it has
been reported \emph{and} its death recorded, that is, once
$\max(X,Y)\le t'-t$. If it has been reported but its death has not been
recorded ($X\le t'-t<Y$), it is shown as alive.

Days of the week are handled as for cases, but separately for the two
delays. The hazard of the reporting delay on day $k$ is multiplied by
$\psi_w$ and that of the delay to a death being recorded by $\psi^{d}_w$,
where $w$ is the day of the week of the next report. This gives effective
survival functions $S^{*}_\theta(t\given t')$ and $S^{*}_\phi(t\given t')$.
The two sets of multipliers have the same prior but are estimated
separately: cases and deaths need not reach those who compile the reports by
the same route or on the same days.

\subsection{What a report shows}

Leaving wrong records aside until \S\ref{sec:wrong}, the cases of onset day
$t$ in the report of day $t_j$ are of three kinds:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & Number & Shown as \\
\midrule
Non-fatal cases reported so far & $s(t\given t_j)$ & alive \\
Fatal cases reported, death not yet recorded & $r(t\given t_j)-m(t\given t_j)$ & alive \\
Fatal cases reported, death recorded & $m(t\given t_j)$ & dead \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $r(t\given t_j)$ is the number of fatal cases reported by day $t_j$.
So
\begin{equation}
C(t\given t_j)=s(t\given t_j)+r(t\given t_j),\qquad M(t\given t_j)=m(t\given t_j).
\label{eq:observed}
\end{equation}
The counts $C$ and $M$ are observed. The number $r$ of fatal cases among
those reported is not: some of the cases shown as alive will die. This is the
one new unobserved quantity for each onset day and report.

\subsection{From one report to the next}

Write $p(t\given t_j)=1-S^{*}_\theta(t\given t_j)/S^{*}_\theta(t\given t_{j-1})$
for the probability that a non-fatal case not reported by $t_{j-1}$ is
reported by $t_j$, as in the existing model, and $p^{F}(t\given t_j)$ for
the same with $S^{*}_{\theta,\nu}$, for a fatal case. Between the reports of
$t_{j-1}$ and $t_j$:
\begin{align}
\text{fatal cases newly reported}\quad
 & a(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t)-r(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p^{F}(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:a}\\
\text{non-fatal cases newly reported}\quad
 & b(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(N(t)-s(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:b}\\
\text{of the $a(t\given t_j)$, death already recorded}\quad
 & x(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(a(t\given t_j),\ 1-S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:x}\\
\text{reported earlier, death now recorded}\quad
 & y(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(r(t\given t_{j-1})-m(t\given t_{j-1}),\
 1-\frac{S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_j)}{S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_{j-1})}\Bigr),
 \label{eq:y}
\end{align}
and then
\[
r(t\given t_j)=r(t\given t_{j-1})+a(t\given t_j),\quad
s(t\given t_j)=s(t\given t_{j-1})+b(t\given t_j),\quad
m(t\given t_j)=m(t\given t_{j-1})+x(t\given t_j)+y(t\given t_j).
\]
All counts are zero before onset. In words: new deaths in a report come from
two sources. Some are fatal cases which were already in the count, shown as
alive, whose death has now been recorded \eqref{eq:y}. The others are fatal
cases entering the count for the first time whose death had been recorded by
then \eqref{eq:x}; for these, being unreported until now says nothing about
whether the death has been recorded, so the probability is simply that $Y$
has elapsed.

Two consequences follow, and both are checks on the construction.
\begin{itemize}
\item When $\nu=0$ the case counts alone behave exactly as in the existing
model: adding \eqref{eq:a} and \eqref{eq:b}, new cases are
$\bin(I(t)-C(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p(t\given t_j))$.
\item A fatal case is shown as dead by day $t'$ with probability
$[1-S^{*}_{\theta,\nu}(t\given t')]\,[1-S^{*}_\phi(t\given t')]$, the
probability that both delays have elapsed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deaths recorded for the day of death}\label{sec:dated}

Some of those who died may be recorded for their day of death and not their
day of onset (for example, where a person was identified only at or after
death and the onset date is not known). Such a case is recorded for the
wrong day: one later than its onset by the time from onset to death. The
model therefore keeps two sets of counts, one by true day of onset and one
by the day cases are recorded for, and moves these cases between them.
Below, a fatal case is \emph{dated by death} if it is recorded for its day
of death and \emph{dated by onset} if it is recorded for its day of onset.

\paragraph{Inputs.} The data cannot tell us which cases these are, so two
things are supplied and not estimated:
\begin{itemize}
\item $f_t$: the fraction of fatal cases with onset on day $t$ which are
dated by death (a single value, or one for
each day; $f_t=0$ gives the model above);
\item $h_z$, $z=1,2,\dots,z_{\max}$: the distribution of the time in days
from onset to death for these cases. It is a gamma distribution with
supplied mean and standard deviation, rounded up to a whole number of days,
so that $h_z$ is the probability the gamma distribution gives to times
between $z-1$ and $z$ days. The time is therefore at least one day: a
person who dies on their day of onset is recorded for the correct day
whichever date is used, and is not counted as dated by death.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{By day of onset.} $I(t)$, $F(t)$ and $c_t$ keep their meanings:
true cases, fatal cases and the case fatality ratio \emph{by true day of
onset}. The renewal model and \eqref{eq:cfr} are unchanged, so that
transmission and the case fatality ratio are both on the correct days. Of
the fatal cases of onset day $t$,
\begin{equation}
D(t)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t),\ f_t\bigr)
\label{eq:dated}
\end{equation}
are dated by their death, and these die after times from onset drawn
independently from $h$: writing $W(t,z)$ for the number of them who die $z$
days after onset,
\begin{equation}
\bigl(W(t,1),\dots,W(t,z_{\max})\bigr)\sim
\operatorname{multinomial}\bigl(D(t),\ h\bigr).
\label{eq:lags}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{By day recorded for.} The cases recorded for day $t$ are then
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & Number & \\
\midrule
Non-fatal cases with onset on day $t$ & $N(t)=I(t)-F(t)$ & \\
Fatal cases with onset on day $t$, dated by onset & $F(t)-D(t)$ & \\
Fatal cases who died on day $t$, dated by death & $G(t)=\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t-z,z)$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
so that $\tilde{F}(t)=F(t)-D(t)+G(t)$ fatal cases and
$\tilde{I}(t)=N(t)+\tilde{F}(t)$ cases in all are recorded for day $t$.

$D(t)$ and $G(t)$ both count fatal cases dated by death, but group them by
different days. $D(t)$ groups them by onset: it is the number with onset on
day $t$, and these are recorded for later days, not for day $t$. $G(t)$
groups them by death: it is the number who died on day $t$, whatever their
day of onset, and these are the ones recorded for day $t$. The two are linked
by $W$, since $D(t)=\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t,z)$.

A fatal case can be recorded for day $t$ in only two ways, which the
expression for $\tilde{F}(t)$ adds up. Either its onset was day $t$ and it
is dated by onset: of the $F(t)$ fatal cases with onset on day $t$, the
$D(t)$ dated by death are recorded for a later day, which leaves
$F(t)-D(t)$. Or it died on day $t$ and is dated by death, its onset being
earlier: there are $G(t)$ of these. No case is counted twice, because a
fatal case is dated either by onset or by death, and none of the $D(t)$
cases is among the $G(t)$, because the time from onset to death is at least
one day.

For example, suppose 10 fatal cases have onset on 9 August, of which 4 are
dated by death, and that 5 fatal cases with earlier onsets, dated by death,
die on 9 August. Then $F=10$, $D=4$ and $G=5$ for 9 August, and
$\tilde{F}=10-4+5=11$ fatal cases are recorded for that day, although only
10 fatal cases truly had onset on it. The 4 are recorded for whichever later
days they die on, and are counted in $G$ for those days.

It is
these, not $F(t)$ and $I(t)$, which the counts $C(t\given t_j)$ and
$M(t\given t_j)$ come to include. In the equations for reports, $t$ is therefore the day cases are recorded
for; how \eqref{eq:a}--\eqref{eq:y} are altered is set out below, first
exactly and then in the approximate form which is used. When $f_t=0$ the two
sets of counts are the same and those equations are unchanged.

\paragraph{How these cases are reported.} A case dated by its death is
assumed to be shown as dead from the first report it appears in. How long
it takes to first appear, counted from its date of death, is set by a
switch:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{supplied} (intended for use): the delay from the date of death
to first appearing in a report has its own gamma distribution, with supplied
mean and standard deviation and survival function $S_\omega$. A person
identified at death is likely to enter the reports quickly, so this would
usually be shorter than the reporting delay of other cases. It is not
estimated and does not change over time. The day-of-week multipliers
$\psi_w$ of the reporting delay apply to it, giving $S^{*}_\omega$;
\item \emph{same as other fatal cases}: the delay from the date of death is
that of other fatal cases from their date of onset, $S_\omega=S_{\theta,\nu}$.
This needs no further input, but there is little reason to believe it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The exact version.} The two kinds of fatal case recorded for day
$t$ are reported at different speeds, so strictly each must be followed
separately. Write $r^{o}(t\given t_j)$ and $r^{d}(t\given t_j)$ for the
numbers reported by day $t_j$ of those dated by onset and those dated by
death, and $m^{o}(t\given t_j)$ for the number of the former shown as dead.
Between the reports of $t_{j-1}$ and $t_j$,
\begin{align}
\text{dated by onset, newly reported}\quad
 & a^{o}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(F(t)-D(t)-r^{o}(t\given t_{j-1}),\ p^{F}(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:ao}\\
\text{dated by death, newly reported}\quad
 & a^{d}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(G(t)-r^{d}(t\given t_{j-1}),\
 1-\frac{S^{*}_\omega(t\given t_j)}{S^{*}_\omega(t\given t_{j-1})}\Bigr),
 \label{eq:ad}\\
\text{of the $a^{o}(t\given t_j)$, death already recorded}\quad
 & x^{o}(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\bigl(a^{o}(t\given t_j),\ 1-S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_j)\bigr),
 \label{eq:xo}
\end{align}
with $y(t\given t_j)$ as in \eqref{eq:y} for the cases dated by onset which
were reported earlier and shown as alive. A case dated by death is shown as
dead in the first report it appears in; one dated by onset is shown as dead
in its first report only if its death has been recorded by then, and
otherwise is shown as alive for now and changes to dead in a later report.
The reports then show
\[
C(t\given t_j)=s(t\given t_j)+r^{o}(t\given t_j)+r^{d}(t\given t_j),\qquad
M(t\given t_j)=m^{o}(t\given t_j)+r^{d}(t\given t_j).
\]
This is exact, but it has two unobserved numbers for each day and report,
$r^{o}$ and $r^{d}$, where the model without these cases has one. The
sampling of these numbers (\S\ref{sec:fitting}) is the slowest part of the
fitting and would be several times slower, by a factor of roughly the
number of cases dated by death recorded for a day.

\paragraph{The approximation used.} The model as built follows only the
total, $r(t\given t_j)=r^{o}(t\given t_j)+r^{d}(t\given t_j)$, as before. To
do this it does not use the number $G(t)$ of cases dated by death as a fixed
number. It treats each of the $\tilde{F}(t)$ fatal cases recorded for day
$t$ as dated by death with probability
\[
\tilde{f}_t=\frac{G(t)}{\tilde{F}(t)},
\]
independently of the others. The probability that such a case has not been
reported by day $t_j$ is then a mixture over the two kinds,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{S}(t\given t_j)=(1-\tilde{f}_t)\,S^{*}_{\theta,\nu}(t\given t_j)
+\tilde{f}_t\,S^{*}_\omega(t\given t_j),
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:ao} and \eqref{eq:ad} are replaced by a single equation for
the fatal cases newly reported, of either kind,
\begin{equation}
a(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(\tilde{F}(t)-r(t\given t_{j-1}),\
1-\frac{\tilde{S}(t\given t_j)}{\tilde{S}(t\given t_{j-1})}\Bigr).
\label{eq:amix}
\end{equation}
Each of these is one of those dated by death with probability
\begin{equation}
\pi(t\given t_j)=\frac{\tilde{f}_t\,\bigl[S^{*}_\omega(t\given t_{j-1})-S^{*}_\omega(t\given t_j)\bigr]}
{\tilde{S}(t\given t_{j-1})-\tilde{S}(t\given t_j)},
\label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
which is large in the first reports after day $t$ if these cases are
reported quickly, and small later. It is then shown as dead in its first
report with certainty if it is dated by death, and with probability
$1-S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_j)$ if it is dated by onset, so that \eqref{eq:xo}
and the cases dated by death together give
\begin{equation}
x(t\given t_j)\sim\bin\Bigl(a(t\given t_j),\
\pi(t\given t_j)+\bigl(1-\pi(t\given t_j)\bigr)\bigl[1-S^{*}_\phi(t\given t_j)\bigr]\Bigr)
\label{eq:xdated}
\end{equation}
for the number newly reported and shown as dead. Equation \eqref{eq:y} is
unchanged, since a fatal case shown as alive cannot be dated by its death.
These are \eqref{eq:a}--\eqref{eq:y} with $\tilde{F}(t)$ for $F(t)$ and two
probabilities altered. The delay $\phi$ is that of the fatal cases dated by
onset only. With the switch at \emph{same as other fatal cases},
$\tilde{S}=S^{*}_{\theta,\nu}$ and $\pi(t\given t_j)=\tilde{f}_t$.

\paragraph{What the approximation changes.} For a single fatal case,
\eqref{eq:mixture}--\eqref{eq:xdated} are exact: a case taken at random from
those recorded for day $t$ is dated by death with probability
$\tilde{f}_t$. The approximation is in treating the cases as independent.
Suppose 10 fatal cases are recorded for a day, of which $G(t)=3$ are dated
by death. Given this, the exact version gives no probability to reports
which would need four or more cases dated by death. The approximation
treats each of the 10 as dated by death with probability $3/10$, so that
the number is 3 on average but could be 2 or 5, and such reports are only
less probable. The expected counts in each report are the same in the two
versions; the approximation allows somewhat more variation about them. It
matters most when counts are small and the two kinds are reported at very
different speeds.

$G(t)$ is itself unobserved and uncertain, and is informed by the reports
in either version, so the approximation does not change what the model can
represent: it weakens how strongly the reports bear on the number of cases
dated by death. Whether this biases any estimate is checked on simulated
data (\S\ref{sec:tests}), which are generated exactly, each case being of
one kind or the other. If it does, the exact version is the remedy.

\paragraph{The start of the series.} Some fatal cases had their onset
before the first day analysed but died after it. If such a case is dated by
death, it is recorded for its day of death, which is inside the period
analysed. For example, with a first day of 12 July, a person with onset on
8 July who died on 15 July and is dated by death is among the cases the
reports count for 15 July.

Such a case must not be taken as a case with onset on 15 July. For onsets
inside the period analysed the model moves a case dated by death back to its
day of onset, but here it cannot: the onset is before the first day, where
the case counts are taken as known and are not changed. So for each of the
first $z_{\max}$ days there is one further unobserved count, $G_0(t)$: the
number of fatal cases recorded for day $t$ whose onset was before the first
day. These are part of $G(t)$,
\[
G(t)=G_0(t)+\sum_{z\geq 1}W(t-z,z),
\]
where the sum is over onsets within the period, and they are left out of
the true cases with onset on day $t$.

The prior for $G_0(t)$ is Poisson, with mean equal to the number of such
cases expected: for each earlier day, the known cases, times the case
fatality ratio $c_1$, times the fraction dated by death, times the
probability under $h$ of dying on day $t$; summed over the earlier days.
After the first $z_{\max}$ days there are none. If no earlier cases are
supplied, there are taken to be none at all. (In updating $c_1$ the
dependence of this prior on it is ignored; it involves only the first few
weeks.)

\paragraph{What is not known.} A case dated by death which has not yet died
is in no report, and once it has died it is recorded for its day of death, not
its day of onset. Recent onset days are therefore missing cases for a reason
other than delay in reporting. The model allows for this: \eqref{eq:lags}
includes deaths after the last day for which there are reports, and the
number of such cases for each recent onset day is a further unobserved
count. But the data say nothing about it, so estimates for recent
days lean on the supplied $f_t$ and $h$. Fitting with a range of values for
both is therefore part of any analysis which uses them.

\subsection{Wrong records}\label{sec:wrong}

The existing model lets records be added to a count in error and later
removed: between two reports $n(t\given t_j)\sim\pois(\eta\,I(t)\,
[S^{*}_\theta(t\given t_{j-1})-S^{*}_\theta(t\given t_j)])$ wrong records are
added, and each wrong record present is removed with probability
$1-(1-\rho)^{t_j-t_{j-1}}$. Since removed records are sometimes dead, a wrong
record is now of one of two kinds: each new wrong record is shown as dead
with probability $\delta$ and as alive otherwise. With $E^{A}$ and $E^{D}$
the numbers of wrong records shown as alive and as dead, \eqref{eq:observed}
becomes
\[
C(t\given t_j)=s(t\given t_j)+r(t\given t_j)+E^{A}(t\given t_j)+E^{D}(t\given t_j),
\qquad
M(t\given t_j)=m(t\given t_j)+E^{D}(t\given t_j).
\]
As now, the total $E^{A}+E^{D}$ for an onset day is limited to a small
number (one more than the largest fall seen in a count of cases or of
deaths). Where some fatal cases are dated by death (\S\ref{sec:dated}), the
$I(t)$ in the rate at which wrong records are added is the number of cases
recorded for day $t$, $\tilde{I}(t)$. A fall in the death
count with no fall in the case count (7 of the 18 falls) needs no further
mechanism: it is a wrong record shown as dead being removed while a genuine
case is added.

\subsection{Changes at unknown times}\label{sec:changes}

Three quantities are piecewise constant in the onset day, each with its own
change points and the same prior on them as in the companion note (each day
starts a new segment with probability $p$, $p\sim\betad(1,T)$, and segments
have a minimum length).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule

the distribution $h$ are inputs.

\section{Assumptions to check}\label{sec:assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The two delays of a fatal case are independent.} In truth a
case found at or near death has both delays short. Independence is what
makes \eqref{eq:x} simple, and is assumed to begin with. The posterior
predictive check for it is the share of reported cases shown as dead by time
since onset, which in the data is 0.41 within 3 days of onset, 0.33 at 3 to
7 days and 0.46 after six weeks. If the fitted model cannot reproduce this,
a dependent mechanism will be explored; the cases of \S\ref{sec:dated} are
one such mechanism, since for them the two delays coincide.
\item \emph{The reporting delays of fatal and non-fatal cases differ, if at
all, by a rescaling of time} (the parameter $\nu$), the same throughout the
outbreak.
\item \emph{A wrong record is as likely to be shown as dead whenever it is
added.}
\item \emph{Cases dated by death are shown as dead from the first report
they appear in} (\S\ref{sec:dated}). The fraction of fatal cases dated in
this way, the distribution of their time from onset to death and the
distribution of their delay from death to first appearing in a report are
supplied, not estimated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What can and cannot be learned}\label{sec:identifiability}

A recent fall in the case fatality ratio and deaths not yet recorded look
the same in the latest reports. For onset days in the last three to four
weeks the death count is far from complete, so $c_t$ there is informed
mostly by the delay $\phi$ estimated from earlier onset days. We therefore
expect that a change in $c_t$ in the last three to four weeks cannot be
detected, and that a change in $\phi$ and a change in $c_t$ at about the same
time will be hard to tell apart. The simulations in \S\ref{sec:tests} are
designed to measure both.

Deaths also inform recent case numbers a little, through \eqref{eq:cfr}:
many early deaths among few reported cases make a small $I(t)$ unlikely.

\section{Fitting}\label{sec:fitting}

The sampler extends the existing one (Metropolis within Gibbs). The new
unobserved counts are the fatal cases $F(t)$ and, for each onset day, the
path over reports of the fatal cases reported $r(t\given t_j)$ and of the
wrong records $E^{A},E^{D}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{$F(t)$, with the path summed out.} Once every case of an onset
day has been reported, the fatal cases among those reported are all the
fatal cases, $r=F(t)$, so neither can move while the other is held fixed.
$F(t)$ is therefore updated first with the path summed out: a change of up
to three is proposed and accepted with the Metropolis probability, using the
probability of the reports of that onset day given $I(t)$ and $F(t)$. This
probability is the result of filtering forwards over the reports.
\item \emph{Paths.} For each onset day the path of
$(r,E^{A},E^{D})$ over reports is then drawn exactly given everything else,
by sampling backwards. The state space is small because $r$ can rise between
two reports by no more than the case count does. Steps 1 and 2 are the
slowest part of the fitting and are done in compiled code; states whose
probability given the reports so far is below $10^{-12}$ of the largest are
dropped.
\item \emph{$I(t)$ and $F(t)$ given the paths.} Given the paths, the
likelihood separates into a term in $F(t)$ from \eqref{eq:a}, a term in
$N(t)$ from \eqref{eq:b} and the renewal terms in $I(t)$. $I(t)$ is drawn
from its discrete conditional with $F(t)$ summed out, and $F(t)$ is then
drawn given $I(t)$, so the pair is drawn jointly. This is what moves the
recent onset days, for which many cases are still to be reported.
\item \emph{Case fatality ratio.} Given $F$ and $I$, the segments of $c_t$
are drawn exactly, with the value in each segment integrated out
(beta-binomial), as for $R_t$ under the Poisson model. The values are then
drawn from their beta conditionals.
\item \emph{Delay to death recorded.} Given the paths, $\phi$ enters only
through \eqref{eq:x} and \eqref{eq:y}. Its segments are updated by the
split, merge and shift moves already used for the reporting delay.
\item \emph{Reporting delay.} As now, with the likelihood from \eqref{eq:a}
and \eqref{eq:b}.
\item \emph{$R_t$ and $\kappa$:} as now.
\item \emph{Wrong records.} Given the paths, the wrong records added and
removed between reports are known. $\delta$ is drawn from its beta
conditional and $\eta$ from its gamma conditional; $\rho$ is updated by a
random walk on the logit scale.
\item \emph{Day-of-week multipliers.} $\psi_w$ and $\psi^{d}_w$ are each
updated by a random walk, given the paths: $\psi_w$ through \eqref{eq:a},
\eqref{eq:b} and the wrong records added, $\psi^{d}_w$ through \eqref{eq:x}
and \eqref{eq:y}.
\item \emph{Cases dated by death} (\S\ref{sec:dated}). Given the paths,
four kinds of change of a single case are proposed, many times in each
iteration, and each is accepted with the Metropolis probability:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{relabel}: one fatal case recorded for day $t$ changes from a
case with onset on day $t$, dated by onset, to a case with onset on day
$t-z$ which died on day $t$, dated by death, or the reverse. This moves one
true case between onset days $t$ and $t-z$;
\item \emph{change of onset}: one case dated by death and recorded for day
$t$ changes its time from onset to death, which moves one true case between
two onset days;
\item \emph{death after the last day}: one fatal case dated by death which
has not yet died is added to, or removed from, a recent onset day;
\item \emph{onset before the first day}: one fatal case recorded for one of
the first days changes from a case with onset on that day to one with
earlier onset, or the reverse.
\end{itemize}
None of these alters the number of cases, or of fatal cases, recorded for
any day. The Metropolis probability involves the renewal model,
\eqref{eq:cfr}, \eqref{eq:dated}, \eqref{eq:lags} and, for the first and
last kinds, the reports of day $t$ through $\tilde{f}_t$. The other updates
are as above with $\tilde{F}(t)$ and the altered probabilities; for days
long enough ago to be taken as fully reported, the true cases with onset on
day $t$ are the latest reported count less $G(t)-D(t)$.
\item \emph{$\nu$.} Given the paths, $\nu$ enters only through
\eqref{eq:a} (and \eqref{eq:pi}, where cases dated by death have their own
delay). From $\nu=0$, a value drawn from the normal part of the prior is
proposed; from $\nu\ne0$, either $\nu=0$ or a small change is proposed,
with equal probability. Each is accepted with the Metropolis--Hastings
probability. The share of draws with $\nu=0$ estimates the posterior
probability that the two delays are the same.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Tests on simulated data}\label{sec:tests}

Data are simulated case by case: each case is given a reporting delay,
whether it dies and, if so, a delay to the death being recorded, and the
reports are counted from these. The tests, each over several simulated
outbreaks and with at least three chains checked by split $\hat{R}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item constant $c_t$ and $\phi$: are $c_t$, $\phi$ and recent $F(t)$
recovered, with no changes inferred?
\item a change in $c_t$ at 6, 4, 3 and 2 weeks before the end of the series:
how old must a change be to be found?
\item a change in $\phi$ with $c_t$ constant: is it attributed to $\phi$ and
not to $c_t$?
\item changes in both;
\item data simulated with the two delays of a fatal case \emph{dependent}:
how far wrong is the model when assumption 1 fails?
\item data simulated with fatal cases reported sooner, and with the same
delay: is $\nu$ recovered in the first case and is $\nu=0$ preferred in the
second?
\item data simulated with a fraction of fatal cases dated by death, fitted with the right inputs, with $f_t=0$, and with a wrong
fraction, a wrong distribution of the time to death or a wrong delay from
death to first appearing in a report: are $R_t$, $c_t$ and
$\phi$ recovered with the right inputs, and how sensitive are they to wrong
ones?
\item cases, $R_t$ and the reporting delay: are these estimated at least as
well as by the model without deaths?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Status}\label{sec:status}

\begin{itemize}
\item Built and unit-tested (package branch \texttt{deaths}): the whole
model of \S\ref{sec:model} and the sampler of \S\ref{sec:fitting}, with the
two sets of counts of \S\ref{sec:dated} in the approximate form; and a
simulator with a change in any of the delays or the case fatality ratio,
fatal cases reported at a different speed, cases dated by death and wrong
records (but not day-of-week effects). The sampling of the paths has been
checked against an independent calculation over every possible path for
small examples, with and without wrong records, and the changes to the
cases dated by death against the full probability of the model. These are
checks that the code does what the equations say, not that the estimates
are right.
\item A first check on simulated data has been run: one simulated outbreak
(60 onset days, about 40 cases a day, reporting delay of mean 8 and sd 6
days, delay to a death being recorded of mean 12 and sd 7 days), with
seven variants of how it is reported, each fitted with the whole model by
two chains of 300 iterations (the first 150 discarded). Table
\ref{tab:first} gives the results. This is a quick check that nothing is
badly wrong, not the tests of \S\ref{sec:tests}: there is one outbreak, the
chains are short, and in two variants the two chains had not yet agreed on
the reporting delay.
\item To do: the tests of \S\ref{sec:tests} with more outbreaks and longer
chains, then the fit with all 49 reports.
\end{itemize}
